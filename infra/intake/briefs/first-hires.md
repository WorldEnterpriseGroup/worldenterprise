# First hires: roles, contracts and onboarding

This brief is about the first person a software company brings on to do work the founders have been doing themselves: what the role is, what kind of relationship it is in law and what has to be on paper before the first day. It is general information, current as of October 11, 2026. It is not legal advice, and it describes United States federal rules only. State and local rules differ and often add to the federal ones, so every point here should be checked with a qualified employment lawyer before a decision rests on it.

## The question this brief answers

A founder who has been doing everything eventually decides that something has to be handed to someone else. The decision usually arrives as a job to be done, such as support, design, a second engineer or someone to run the books, and the practical questions follow quickly. Should this person be an employee or a contractor? What should they sign? What does the company owe them, and what do they owe the company? What has to happen before they start?

This brief walks through those questions in the order a founder meets them. It reports what public sources from United States agencies and statutes say, separates that from analysis of how a young company might use it, and says plainly where the sources stop. It does not recommend a type of role, a salary or a hiring plan.

A first hire is a legal relationship before it is a job title, and the relationship is decided by the facts of the work and not by what the paperwork calls it.

## About the evidence

This brief draws on ten documents, each retrieved and read on October 11, 2026. They fall into two groups.

The first group is federal agency guidance. The Internal Revenue Service publishes three pages used here: one on how to tell an independent contractor from an employee, one on hiring employees and one on the forms and taxes that apply to independent contractors. The Department of Labor publishes a page on misclassification, a fact sheet on the employment relationship under the Fair Labor Standards Act and a page on overtime. U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services publishes the page for Form I-9, and the Small Business Administration publishes a guide to hiring and managing employees.

The second group is statute. Cornell Law School's Legal Information Institute publishes the text of two sections of the federal copyright code: the definitions, including the meaning of a work made for hire, and the rule on how copyright ownership is transferred.

Beyond these sources is analysis, which is the brief's own. Where a sentence is a judgment and not a report of a source, it is marked as analysis or phrased as a consideration. No source in the set reports how often young companies misclassify a worker, what a first hire costs or how long onboarding takes, so the brief gives no statistic about any of them.

Three limits apply throughout. The agency pages are summaries for a general audience and leave out exceptions. All of the material is federal, and state employment law, which can be stricter, is outside it. And none of the sources addresses equity compensation, immigration sponsorship, benefits or hiring outside the United States.

## Choose the role before the contract

Analysis: a founder who begins with the contract tends to write the contract that fits the person across the table. A more useful order starts with the work.

A short description of the role answers four things.

- What the person will produce, in terms someone else could check.
- Who decides how the work is done, and who decides what is done.
- Whether the person will work for others at the same time, and with whose tools and equipment.
- Whether the need is a continuing part of the business or a defined project with an end.

The answers matter because they are also the facts that the law uses to classify the relationship. A role described honestly in these terms usually shows which kind of relationship it is. A role that cannot be described without contradiction, such as full-time hours set by the company and an arrangement that says the person is a contractor, is the kind that causes trouble later.

Analysis: it also helps to separate the work from the person. A founder might hope that one early hire will cover engineering, support and sales. A written description of each function, even if one person covers three, shows which parts of the role can be handed on later.

## Employee or contractor

The question of whether a worker is an employee or an independent contractor does not have one test in federal law. Two agencies publish guidance that a company can read.

**The Internal Revenue Service.** The agency's page says that "Facts that provide evidence of the degree of control and independence fall into three categories:" behavioral control, financial control and the type of relationship. For behavioral control it asks, "Does the company control or have the right to control what the worker does and how the worker does his or her job?" For financial control it asks whether the business aspects of the worker's job are controlled by the payer. For the type of relationship it asks about written contracts and employee-type benefits, and about whether the relationship will continue and the work is a key aspect of the business. The page also says that "all information that provides evidence of the degree of control and independence must be considered."

**The Department of Labor.** Its fact sheet on the employment relationship under the Fair Labor Standards Act says that employee status "is determined by looking at the economic realities of the worker’s relationship with the employer." It says that "What the worker is called is not relevant" and goes on to say that a worker may be an employee whatever the label. It lists factors that are looked at together: opportunity for profit or loss depending on managerial skill, investments by the worker and the employer, permanence of the work relationship, nature and degree of control, whether the work is integral to the employer's business, and skill and initiative.

The Department's page on misclassification states, "Misclassification occurs when an employer treats a worker who is an employee under the FLSA as an independent contractor." The Small Business Administration makes a similar point more briefly: "Independent contractors can sometimes qualify as employees in a legal sense."

Two things follow from these sources. The first is that a signed statement calling someone a contractor does not settle the matter, because both agencies look at how the relationship works in practice. The second is that the agencies use different tests. The Internal Revenue Service considers control and independence in three categories, and the Department of Labor looks at economic reality in six factors. A worker can be a contractor under one test and an employee under the other, and state law adds further tests.

Analysis: for a first hire, the two questions that tend to decide the matter are the ones the sources return to. Does the company control how the work is done? And is the work a continuing, central part of the business? A founder who answers yes to both should expect that the worker is an employee, whatever the contract says, and should plan the payroll and the paperwork that follow. A founder who answers no to both may have a real contractor relationship, which is a matter for counsel to confirm.

Analysis: the cost of getting it wrong falls mainly on the company, because the company is the party that failed to withhold tax or to pay what the law required. The sources describe the consequence only in general terms and this brief does not set out penalties.

## Overtime and how the job is classified

Employee status raises a second question, which is whether the employee is entitled to overtime. The Department of Labor's page on overtime says that "Unless exempt, employees covered by the Act must receive overtime pay for hours worked over 40 in a workweek" and goes on to state the rate of pay. The page also links to a separate page on earnings thresholds for the executive, administrative and professional exemption, which suggests that salary level is one of the conditions for that exemption. It does not set out the duties or salary tests, so the brief does not either.

Analysis: three consequences follow for a first hire.

- A job title does not make a role exempt. The page refers to exemptions that depend on conditions, and a founder should expect that a title such as manager or engineer does not settle the question by itself.
- A non-exempt employee's hours have to be recorded, and a company with no timekeeping habit will need to start one.
- A role in which the person works long hours in a launch period is the situation in which this question becomes expensive, because the overtime applies to the hours worked and not to the hours the offer letter assumed.

The classification of a role as exempt or non-exempt is a legal conclusion that depends on duties, pay and state rules. It should be made by someone who has read the current regulations and the state's own requirements, not copied from a job posting.

## What the written agreement covers

A written agreement does not decide whether someone is an employee, but it carries a company's other needs. Analysis: for a software company the contents that matter most are these.

**The role and the terms.** A description of the role, the pay and its schedule, the start date and the manager. For an employee, any statement that employment can end at either side's choice is a state-law matter for counsel. For a contractor, a defined scope, a payment schedule and a term.

**Ownership of the work.** This is the clause a software company can least afford to leave out, and the statutes explain why. The federal copyright code defines a work made for hire as "a work prepared by an employee within the scope of his or her employment." It gives a second, narrower route for specially commissioned works that fall into listed categories, and it requires that in those cases the parties "expressly agree in a written instrument signed by them" that the work is made for hire.

A separate section deals with transfers. It says, "A transfer of copyright ownership, other than by operation of law, is not valid unless an instrument of conveyance" or a note or memorandum of the transfer is in writing and signed by the owner of the rights conveyed or the owner's authorized agent.

Analysis: two things follow. For an employee, the statute's own rule supplies a default for work done within the scope of the job, and the agreement is where the company says clearly what that scope covers, including work done at home or on a personal machine. For a contractor, the work does not become the company's by default, so a signed assignment is the usual way to move it, and whether a particular piece of software fits one of the statute's listed categories is a question for counsel. In both cases, an assignment that is signed after the work is finished is better than none, and one signed before the work starts is better still.

**Confidentiality.** What the person may not disclose, and for how long after the relationship ends. The sources here do not address it, so the content is a matter for counsel.

**Restrictions after leaving.** Some states limit what a company may ask a departing worker to agree to about competing work. The sources do not cover those rules, and this is a clause to take to a lawyer who knows the state where the worker is located.

**What the agreement leaves to other documents.** An offer of equity needs its own instrument and its own tax treatment. This brief does not cover it.

## The paperwork on the first day

Hiring an employee brings a short set of federal obligations. They are modest in number and easy to forget when the first hire is also the first time the company has had to do any of them.

**Work authorization.** The page for Form I-9 says that "All U.S. employers must properly complete Form I-9 for every individual they hire for employment in the United States." It also says that "Both employees and employers (or authorized representatives of the employer) must complete the form." The page states a retention rule: the employer keeps the form "for three years after the date of hire, or for one year after employment is terminated, whichever is later", and the page gives this in a list of employer steps. The Internal Revenue Service says that an "Employer must verify the identity and employment authorization of individuals hired for employment in the United States." The page on Form I-9 does not state the deadline for completing each section, so a founder should read the form's instructions for the timing.

**Tax withholding.** The Internal Revenue Service's page says, "Ask all new employees to give you a signed Form W-4 when they start work." It also says, "You are required to get each employee's name and Social Security number (SSN) and to enter them on Form W-2" and adds that the requirement also applies to resident and nonresident alien employees. The Small Business Administration's guide lists, among the steps to hire an employee, that the company should "Get an Employer Identification Number (EIN)" and should know which records must stay on file and for how long.

**State requirements.** The sources describe federal obligations. Many states require further steps, such as reporting new hires, workers' compensation coverage and posted notices. None of the sources addresses these, so a company should ask its payroll provider or counsel for the state list.

Analysis: the sensible habit is a single folder for each employee, created on the first day, containing the signed agreement, the verification form and the tax form, with the retention date written on the outside. The company that does this once for its first hire has a template for its tenth.

## Paying a contractor

A contractor relationship has its own, smaller set of tasks. The Internal Revenue Service's page on the forms for independent contractors says that Form W-9 "can be used to request the correct name and taxpayer identification number" of the payee. It says that the payer must withhold at a stated backup withholding rate from reportable payments of nonemployee compensation in some situations, and it says that Form 1099-NEC is used to report payments to contractors, and, generally, is not used to report wages and other compensation paid to employees. The page does not state the dollar amount at which reporting begins. It refers the reader to the form instructions.

Analysis: a company that engages a contractor should ask for the Form W-9 before the first payment, not after the first invoice, and should keep it with the agreement. A contractor who objects to providing it is telling the company something about the arrangement.

The page is a summary of the tax forms. It does not address how the contractor's classification is decided, which is the subject of the earlier section, and a Form 1099-NEC does not make a worker a contractor.

## Onboarding the first hire

Onboarding is a company's process for turning a signed agreement into someone who can do the work. The sources address the paperwork half. The rest is analysis.

Analysis: a first hire arrives in a company that has no habits, because nobody has needed any. A useful week-one plan has four parts.

- **Access.** The accounts, repositories and tools the person needs, granted on the first day and recorded, with a matching record of what must be taken back if they leave.
- **Context.** A short written account of what the company is trying to do, who its customers are and what the founders have learned so far. It is the same document that a later hire will need.
- **A first piece of real work.** A task that is small, well defined and finished within the first days, so that the person and the company both learn how the other works.
- **A conversation at the end of the first week.** The founder asks what was unclear, and writes down what the person says. The list is a map of what the company has been carrying in its head.

The sources do not say how long onboarding takes or what a good one looks like, and this brief does not offer a standard. It suggests only that whatever the first hire needs to be told should be written down once, because the same information will be needed again.

## Questions to ask counsel

These questions are for an employment lawyer who knows the state where the worker is located.

**Is this person an employee or a contractor on the facts of the work?** Ask under the federal tests and the state's.

**Is the role exempt or non-exempt, and what must we record?** The answer controls overtime and timekeeping.

**What does the agreement have to say about ownership of the work?** Ask how it should treat work done before the agreement was signed.

**Which restrictions after leaving are lawful in the worker's state?** A clause that is lawful in one state may not be in another.

**What state steps apply to a first hire?** Ask for the full list, including coverage and notices.

**If we offer equity, what has to be signed and filed?** The instrument and its tax treatment need separate advice.

## What this brief cannot tell you

This brief cannot tell you whether a particular worker is an employee or a contractor. The agencies use different tests, state law adds others, and each test turns on facts about your relationship that no general document can know.

It cannot tell you what to pay, what to offer or whom to hire. No source in the set addresses compensation, and the brief offers no figure for it.

It cannot tell you the state rules. Everything here is federal. State law on classification, overtime, notices, coverage and restrictions after leaving can differ, and it applies to many first hires.

It cannot tell you how to structure equity, immigration sponsorship, benefits or hiring outside the United States. These subjects are outside the evidence.

It cannot tell you how a rule applies to your facts. The pages are summaries for a general audience, and the statute sections are quoted in part. A lawyer who reads the full text and your documents can say what applies.

It does not describe the clients, results or terms of WE.

Finally, it contains analysis, and the analysis can be wrong. The sections above mark where a sentence is a judgment. Treat those sentences as questions to test with counsel and with the person you are hiring.
