# From the first ten customers to the first hundred

This brief is about the stretch of a software company's life in which sales stop being a series of personal favors and start needing a method. It is general information, current as of October 11, 2026. It is not legal, tax or investment advice, and the messaging rules it describes should be read in their current text, with counsel, before a campaign depends on them.

## The question this brief answers

Most founders can find ten customers. They ask friends of friends, answer every inquiry themselves, say yes to unusual requests and sit in the product's first weeks beside each new user. The effort is real, but nothing about it is repeatable, because it depends on who the founders happen to know and on how many hours they have in a day.

The question this brief takes up is what has to change for the next ninety. It does not offer a number of calls to make or a pipeline ratio to hit. It asks instead what the first ten customers can teach, which channels reach more people like them, what the law expects before a message is sent to a stranger, and how to tell whether a method is working or only keeping the founders busy.

The first ten customers are found by effort and the next ninety are found by a process, and the work between them is learning which parts of the effort can be written down.

## About the evidence

This brief draws on seven documents, each retrieved and read on October 11, 2026. They are less numerous in this field than in a field governed by statute, and the reader should know what kind of support each one gives.

The first group is government material. The Small Business Administration describes market research and competitive analysis for a new business. The Federal Trade Commission publishes a compliance guide for the CAN-SPAM Act, which governs commercial email in the United States, and a set of answers about its endorsement guides, which govern how reviews and customer quotes may be used in marketing. The Canadian government explains its anti-spam legislation in plain language, and the United Kingdom's Information Commissioner's Office explains how its electronic marketing rules treat email to individuals and to companies.

The second group is two practitioner publications. The Lean Startup site sets out a method for learning from customers, and it is the work of a particular school of thought, so it is used here only to describe that method. Stripe's documentation explains how a payment provider models a subscription. It is vendor documentation, used only to describe how that product works, and nothing in this brief endorses it.

The third group is analysis. Where a sentence is a judgment and not a report of a source, it is marked as analysis or phrased as a consideration. No source in the set measures how many companies reach a hundred customers or by what route, so this brief offers no statistic about growth, conversion or time, and it does not describe a typical path.

Two limits are worth stating at the start. The messaging pages are summaries for a general audience and leave out the exceptions that the underlying laws contain. And the legal sources cover the United States, Canada and the United Kingdom only. A company selling in the European Union or elsewhere faces other rules that no page here describes.

## What changes between ten and a hundred

Analysis: three things change at the same time, and founders often notice only one of them.

The first is the source of customers. Early customers come through the founders' own contacts. Later customers come through channels the founders do not personally own, such as search, partners, outbound lists and the recommendations of people who have only heard of the company. A channel can be tested, measured and handed to someone else, which a friendship cannot.

The second is who does the work. At ten customers, the founders are the sales team, the support desk and the onboarding guide. At a hundred, someone else will answer the first message, and what that person says needs to exist in writing before the day it is needed.

The third is the cost of a mistake. A founder who misjudges a conversation with one customer loses one customer. A message template that is wrong, or a list that was assembled without regard to the rules on consent, repeats the error across hundreds of recipients before anyone notices.

None of this means that the founders should stop selling personally. It means that the personal selling is now also an experiment, and that its results should be recorded so that someone else can repeat what worked.

## Know who the first ten were

Before searching for the next ninety, it helps to describe the first ten precisely. The Small Business Administration says that "Market research helps you find customers for your business." Its page also says that "Competitive analysis helps you learn from businesses competing for your potential customers." The agency's guidance on how to learn about customers includes gathering demographic information and, for specific insights, surveying or interviewing potential buyers directly.

Those sentences are written for a general small business, and a software company will add its own questions. Analysis: a useful description of the first ten customers answers six of them in the customers' own terms.

- What problem was each of them trying to solve on the day they said yes?
- What were they doing about it before the product existed?
- Who in their organization felt the problem, and who signed?
- What nearly stopped the purchase?
- Which part of the product do they use every week, and which part have they never opened?
- How did they hear about the company?

The last question matters most for the search. If seven of the ten came through the founders' own network, the founders know their network well and know little yet about any other channel. That is a fact worth writing down in those words, because it prevents a method from being credited with results that a friendship produced.

Analysis: differences among the first ten are information too. If two customers use the product in a way the founders did not plan, the company may have two markets and not one. Choosing which of them to pursue is a decision to make deliberately, and it is easier to make while the group is small enough to be described one customer at a time.

## Find the next ninety

A channel is a repeatable way of reaching a particular kind of buyer. The brief does not rank channels, because the sources do not, and the right one depends on the buyer and the price. It describes four common families and what each asks of the company.

**Referrals from existing customers.** The cost is mostly attention. A customer who is glad to be asked has to be asked in a way that is easy to act on, and the company has to make it plain what it wants, which is usually an introduction to one specific kind of colleague. Analysis: referral volume tends to track the number of satisfied customers, so a referral channel is rarely the way out of a shortage of customers. It is a result of having good ones.

**Outbound messages to a defined list.** The company chooses who should be asked and writes to them. This channel can be started on any day and stopped on any day, which makes it good for testing a message. It is also the channel with the most rules, which the next section covers.

**Inbound interest from content and search.** The company publishes something a buyer would look for and lets the buyer find it. The cost is up front and the return is slow. Analysis: it suits products with a problem that buyers already search for by name, and it suits poorly a product that has to teach buyers what the problem is.

**Partners and marketplaces.** Another organization, which already has the attention of the buyers, mentions or sells the product. The cost is a dependency. The partner sets the terms of the listing, and a change in those terms changes the company's pipeline without warning. A written agreement with any partner is a matter for counsel.

For each family, the same question applies. If the company does the same thing again next month, will the same kind of customer arrive? A channel that works once has taught the company something. A channel that works twice has become a method.

## Outreach rules that apply before you send

Outbound email is the quickest channel to start and the one where a hasty start causes the most harm. Three sets of rules are described here, and each is a summary that leaves out detail.

**The United States.** The Federal Trade Commission's compliance guide lists the main requirements of the CAN-SPAM Act in short imperative sentences. They are "Don’t use false or misleading header information." Then "Identify the message as an ad." Then "Tell recipients where you’re located." Then "Tell recipients how to opt out of receiving future marketing email from you." Then "Honor opt-out requests promptly." And finally "Monitor what others are doing on your behalf." The guide also says that the Act applies to all commercial messages and adds, "The law makes no exception for business-to-business email."

The last point matters to a company that sells to other companies, because it is a common belief that rules about unwanted email protect consumers only. The agency's own guide says otherwise. The sixth requirement matters to a company that hires an agency or a contractor to send messages, because the company remains responsible for what is sent in its name.

**Canada.** The Canadian government's page on its anti-spam legislation describes the law as requesting, among other things, "that businesses obtain consent before sending commercial electronic messages." The page is an introduction and states that it is not legal advice. It does not set out the exact conditions for consent, and the full text of the Act is linked from it. Analysis: a company that sends to recipients in Canada should treat consent as something to establish before the first message, and should have counsel read the Act itself.

**The United Kingdom.** The Information Commissioner's Office says that "You must not send marketing emails or texts to individuals without specific consent." It describes a limited exception for a company's own previous customers, often called the soft opt-in, and says that this exception "does not apply to prospective customers or new contacts." It also says that "You can send marketing emails or texts to companies." It adds that sole traders and some partnerships are treated as individuals. A software company selling to very small businesses can therefore find that some of its recipients are individuals in law although they are businesses in practice.

Analysis: the three rule sets do not agree, and a company that wants one list and one template will end up following the strictest of them for everyone. A simpler practice is to keep a record, for each address, of where it came from and what the person agreed to, so that the answer to a regulator or a recipient is a lookup and not an investigation.

These sources describe email. Telephone calls, text messages, direct mail and messages sent through social platforms each have rules of their own that are not covered here, and neither are the data protection laws that govern how a list may be collected in the first place.

## Using customer words and reviews honestly

By the time a company has ten customers, some of them will have said kind things about it, and the founders will want to put those words on the website. The Federal Trade Commission's answers about its endorsement guides set some limits. They state that endorsements "must be honest and not misleading." They also state, "You can’t talk about your experience with a product if you haven’t tried it."

On disclosure, the agency says that a connection between a business and an endorser which the audience would not expect "should be disclosed clearly and conspicuously." It adds, "The same is usually true if the endorser has been paid or given something of value to tout the product."

Analysis: for a young company, these sentences point to three habits.

- Quote a customer only with that customer's agreement, and keep the agreement in writing so that the words can be removed if the customer asks.
- Do not edit a quote so that it says more than the customer said.
- If a customer received something of value, such as a discount in return for a review, say so beside the review and not in a separate place.

The guide covers much more than this, including influencer marketing and the use of reviews on third-party sites, and a company that plans to rely on any of it should read the guide through. The point here is narrower. Proof from early customers is the most persuasive thing a young company has, which is exactly why it is worth handling so that it can be checked.

## Turn a sale into a repeatable one

A process is a way of doing the same thing the same way whoever does it. For selling, the minimum written record is short.

- Who the company sells to, in one paragraph, and who it does not sell to.
- The three or four questions that a first conversation must answer.
- The next step after each possible answer.
- What a signed customer receives first, and from whom.
- The price and the terms in force, and who may change them.

Analysis: the last item is the one founders skip. When the price lives in the founder's head, it drifts toward whatever the last customer would pay, and the company discovers at fifty customers that it has fifty prices. Writing the terms down does not make them rigid. It makes each exception visible, so that a discount is a decision and not an accident.

Billing is part of the process. Stripe's documentation explains that "Subscriptions let customers make recurring payments to access a product or service." It also explains that when a subscription is created, the provider "automatically generates invoices, attempts payment collection, and manages the subscription status throughout its lifecycle." The document describes the states a subscription moves through, including a trial period that delays the first charge. Analysis: a company that records which state each customer is in, and what the product does in each state, avoids the common surprise of discovering that customers have been using the product for weeks without paying, or have been billed after they left.

The documentation describes one vendor's product and is not a recommendation of it. The idea that carries over to any provider is that the commercial relationship has a lifecycle, and the lifecycle should be designed before the hundredth customer and not discovered after it.

## Measure learning, not activity

At ten customers, the founders know every account. At a hundred, they know only the numbers they have chosen to keep. The Lean Startup site names a way of choosing them. It says that "The unit of progress for Lean Startups is validated learning" and that this learning "can be validated scientifically, by running experiments that allow us to test each element of our vision." It describes "the build-measure-learn feedback loop" as a core component of the method, and it says startup accounting "must include actionable metrics."

The site does not define an actionable metric in the sentences quoted here, so the following is analysis. A metric is actionable when a change in it would lead the team to do something different. The count of messages sent is easy to keep and changes nothing. The count of customers who reached a first useful result in the product is harder to keep and tells the team whether the sales channel is bringing in people the product can serve.

A modest set of measures, kept the same way each month, is enough.

- How many people each channel reached, and how many of them became customers.
- How many customers each channel produced who were still active at a later date the company chooses in advance.
- What each channel cost in money and in founder time.
- What customers said, in their own words, when they chose to leave.

Analysis: a group of customers that arrived in the same month through the same channel is worth looking at together, because a method can look good on average while one month's customers behave very differently from the next. This brief does not set a target for any of these figures, because no source supports one.

Keep each experiment small enough to stop. Say in advance what result would make the company continue the channel and what result would make it stop. A channel that is judged only after months of effort tends to be continued out of reluctance to have wasted them.

## Questions to ask before you scale a channel

These questions are for the founders and, where they involve a contract or a regulation, for counsel.

**Who exactly will we write to, and where did we get their addresses?** The answer determines which rules apply, and it should be written down, not remembered.

**What does the recipient agree to, and can we show it?** Consent and opt-out records are the evidence in any complaint.

**Who sends on our behalf, and what do we know about what they send?** A contractor's mistake is the company's problem.

**Which of our early customers' words are we using, and do we have their agreement?** Proof that cannot be checked is a liability.

**What happens to a customer in each billing state?** The product should behave on purpose in each one.

**What would make us stop?** A channel with no stopping rule never stops.

**What is the next ninety customers' first useful result?** If the product cannot deliver the first result without a founder in the room, scaling the channel will scale the wait.

## What this brief cannot tell you

This brief cannot tell you which channel to use. That depends on your buyer, your price and your product, and the sources here do not rank channels.

It cannot tell you how many customers a channel will bring, how long the first hundred will take or what a good conversion rate is. No source in the set measures those things, and the brief offers no figure for them. Nothing in it should be read as a promise that any method will produce customers.

It cannot tell you whether your messages are lawful. The email sources are summaries for a general audience. The United States, Canadian and British rules differ, and none of the pages describes how the rules apply to a particular company. Rules for telephone calls, text messages, social platforms and data protection are outside the sources, as are the rules of the European Union and every other jurisdiction not named.

It cannot tell you how to price. The brief says that the terms should be written down, and it does not say what they should be.

It cannot tell you how a particular payment provider will behave for your account. The vendor documentation describes the general model, and the details of any account depend on its settings and on the provider's current terms.

It does not describe the clients, results or terms of WE.

Finally, it contains analysis, and the analysis can be wrong. The sections above mark where a sentence is a judgment. Treat those sentences as hypotheses to test with your own first ten customers.
