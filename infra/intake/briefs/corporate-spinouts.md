# Building or spinning out a software product inside a company

This brief is written for a team inside an established company that is considering a new software product and has to decide where it will live. It sets out four routes and asks of each what the company keeps, what it gives up and what has to be written down. It is general information, current as of October 11, 2026. It is not legal, tax or investment advice, and every rule it describes should be checked against the current text with a qualified professional before a decision rests on it.

## The question this brief answers

A company that already sells to customers and employs people often has an idea that does not fit its main business. The question is rarely whether to build it. The question is where the work should sit, because the answer changes who owns the code, who can be paid in shares, who votes on the big decisions and which public programs the new venture can ever use.

The sources in this brief suggest the choice is more legal than organizational. A corporate spinout is a negotiation about who owns the software, who controls the company and who answers for the people long before it is a product decision.

This brief therefore asks the same questions of every route. Who owns what the team writes? How are the people employed, and can they hold shares? What does the parent keep? Which rules count the parent's size or ownership against the new venture? What does the parent's own tax position look like if shares leave the parent? What has to be separated, in systems and in data? It describes how these questions work in general. It names no company, describes no particular arrangement and claims no corporate client.

## About the evidence

This brief draws on fifteen documents, each retrieved and read on the day it was written, October 11, 2026. Most are statute and regulation as published by Cornell Law School's Legal Information Institute: a Securities and Exchange Commission rule, Small Business Administration rules, sections of the Internal Revenue Code, and the copyright and patent statutes. The rest are agency and government pages on size standards, stock options, patent essentials, cybersecurity and Delaware's director provisions.

Where a sentence is a judgment rather than a report of a source, it is marked as analysis. No outcome data about spinouts appears in the evidence set, so this brief offers none: no count of ventures that survived, no measure of how long a separation takes and no comparison of routes by results.

Three limits belong at the start. First, all of the material describes United States federal rules, plus the law of one state, Delaware. Second, rules in this area change, and a page read on one day can be out of date by the next. Third, some of the sources are summaries, and a summary leaves out exceptions that the rule itself contains.

## How to read the routes

The four routes below are ordered from the most attached to the parent to the least. Each is described by its mechanism, not by its likelihood of working, and the routes combine: a venture may start as an internal team, move into a subsidiary and later take outside investors, and the written terms at each step either carry forward or have to be redone.

## Route one: a team inside the parent

The simplest route keeps the product inside the existing company, with a small team under the parent's employment contracts.

The copyright statute explains why ownership is usually settled here. It says that copyright vests initially in the author, and that for a work made for hire the employer is treated as the author. Its definition of a work made for hire begins with the plainest case, a work prepared by an employee within the scope of his or her employment. Analysis: for code written by salaried staff as part of their jobs, this suggests that the parent is the author without further paper, though the scope of employment is where disputes arise.

Patents work differently, because the right to apply starts with the inventor. The Patent and Trademark Office's page on essentials says that the inventor or the inventor's legal representative may apply for a patent, and that a person to whom an inventor has assigned an invention may also apply. Analysis: a company that wants to own what its employees invent usually needs a written assignment or an obligation to assign.

The route gives up speed and flexibility, because the product inherits the parent's hiring, pay and approval chain. Analysis: it suits a product close to the core business and less well one that needs to pay people in shares, raise outside money or be sold on its own.

## Route two: a separate company owned by the parent

A second route puts the product into a new legal entity that the parent owns in whole and that holds the code and the contracts.

The first question is what moves. Patents are assignable by a written instrument, according to the patent statute's section on ownership. It says an assignment is void against a later buyer or lender who paid value without notice unless it is recorded, and it sets a three-month window, running from the date of the assignment, for recording it. Analysis: an assignment into a new entity is paperwork to be signed and recorded on purpose, not a side effect of the reorganization.

Copyright can be moved too. The copyright statute says that ownership may be transferred in whole or in part by any means of conveyance or by operation of law. Analysis: whether to transfer code or license it to the new entity is a real choice, and the fourth route takes it up again.

The entity also gets its own governance. The Delaware General Corporation Law says that the business and affairs of a corporation are managed by or under the direction of a board of directors, unless the chapter or the certificate of incorporation provides otherwise. The same subchapter allows the board, by resolution passed by a majority of the whole board, to designate committees. Analysis: a wholly owned subsidiary still has directors, and a parent that expects to run it by instruction should ask counsel what the director provisions allow.

Analysis: this route suits a venture that needs a clean boundary, such as its own contracts or liabilities, while the parent remains its only owner. It cannot yet pay people in shares in a meaningful way, because the only shareholder is the parent.

## Route three: a spinout with outside investors

A third route brings in other owners. The new company issues shares to outside investors and to the employees who move across, and the parent keeps a majority or a minority stake.

Each step has a securities question. Shares for outside investors are the subject of other exemptions, which this brief does not cover. Shares for the people who work there are covered by Rule 701. The rule says that offers and sales made in compliance with all of its conditions are exempt from section 5 of the Securities Act, which is the registration requirement. It is available to an issuer that is not subject to the reporting requirements of section 13 or 15(d) of the Exchange Act. The rule also names who may take part and caps the amount sold in a consecutive 12-month period, and its text should be read for both.

The tax rules on pay in shares are a separate layer. The Internal Revenue Service's topic page on stock options separates statutory options from other options. For the statutory kind, it says that you generally do not include any amount in gross income when you receive or exercise the option, and that taxable income or a deductible loss arises when you sell the stock. For options that are not statutory and have no readily determinable value, it says no income is recognized at grant and the fair market value of the stock is included in income when the option is exercised.

Section 83 of the Internal Revenue Code covers property transferred in connection with services. The general rule is that the person who performed the services includes the excess of the property's value over any amount paid in gross income in the first year in which the rights become transferable or are no longer subject to a substantial risk of forfeiture. The same section lets that person elect instead to include the amount in the year of the transfer, and it sets the election's deadline at no later than 30 days after the date of the transfer. Analysis: a deadline that short, written into the statute, is a reason to decide how employees will receive shares before the transfer, not after it.

Section 409A of the Code concerns nonqualified deferred compensation plans. It provides that when such a plan fails the section's requirements, deferred compensation that is not subject to a substantial risk of forfeiture is included in gross income. Analysis: bonuses, phantom shares or delayed payments for people who move from the parent are a reason to ask counsel whether the section reaches them.

Analysis: this route suits a venture that needs outside money and wants its people to share in what they build, and it asks the most of the parent, because every earlier question now has several owners.

## Route four: licensing the technology out

A fourth route keeps the parent as the owner of the intellectual property and lets another company build and sell the product under a license.

This route turns the ownership question into a drafting question: the license has to say what the licensee may do and what happens to improvements. The patent statute shows why the second point matters. It says that, unless the owners agree otherwise, each joint owner of a patent may exploit the invention without the consent of and without accounting to the other owners. Analysis: if the parent and the licensee jointly own an improvement and the agreement is silent, each of them may be able to use it, and to license it to someone else, without asking or paying the other. The contract should make that choice on purpose.

Analysis: this route suits a parent that wants the technology used but not to run the venture, and it gives control of the product's direction only through the license terms.

## Who owns the software

Every route depends on a short list of facts about ownership. For code written by employees, the work made for hire definition applies to a work prepared within the scope of employment. For code written by someone who is not an employee, the definition's second paragraph covers a work specially ordered or commissioned, but only for listed categories of work. Analysis: a contractor who wrote part of the product may not have produced a work made for hire at all, in which case the copyright vests in the contractor as author until a written transfer says otherwise. A team should find these gaps before the venture signs a customer contract that promises the venture owns its code. For inventions, the starting point is the inventor and the sources point to written instruments. This brief has no source on open source or third-party components, or on state limits on employee invention agreements, and leaves both to counsel.

## Moving people and giving them equity

People are the part of a spinout that is hardest to undo. The securities rule for compensation, Rule 701, lets certain issuers offer shares to employees without registering the offer, and it limits who can receive them and how much can be sold in a 12-month period. The tax pages show that the same grant can be taxed at several points, depending on whether it is a statutory option, another option or a transfer of shares.

Analysis: three timing facts collide in a typical move. The parent's invention agreements decide what the employee already owes the parent. The new entity's share plan decides what the employee will own. The tax deadlines decide when the employee has to act. A plan that sequences them in the wrong order can leave an employee with a tax bill or the company without an assignment.

## What the parent keeps

Control has two layers: the control the parent holds by owning shares, and the control it holds by contract. By shares, the Delaware director provisions place management with the board, subject to the certificate of incorporation. A parent that owns all the shares of a subsidiary elects its board. A parent that owns a minority may hold board seats, special voting rights or approval rights that the certificate of incorporation or an agreement creates. Analysis: in a venture with outside investors, the lists of decisions that need each owner's consent are among the first things each party reads, and every item is a restriction on the people running the venture.

By contract, the parent can keep services agreements, a license of its technology to the venture and a license back of the venture's improvements. Analysis: each ties the venture to the parent, and each can be a reason a later investor or buyer asks for changes.

## Small-business programs and corporate ownership

One consequence of ownership surprises many parents: the size and eligibility rules of public programs can look through a company to its owners.

The Small Business Administration's page on size standards says that size standards define the largest size a business can be to participate in government contracting programs, and that you must include the employees or receipts of all affiliates when determining the size of a business. It adds that affiliation with another business is based on the power to control, whether exercised or not. The affiliation rule in the regulations says that concerns and entities are affiliates of each other when one controls or has the power to control the other, or a third party controls or has the power to control both. It states that it does not matter whether control is exercised, so long as the power to control exists. The same rule describes how stock ownership is read: a holder of 50 percent or more of a concern's voting stock controls or has the power to control it, and options and convertible securities are treated as though the rights granted have been exercised.

For the SBIR and STTR programs, the eligibility rule has its own ownership test. It says that a concern must be more than 50% directly owned and controlled by one or more individuals, and the individuals must be citizens or permanent residents of the United States. A separate paragraph allows concerns owned by venture capital operating companies, hedge funds or private equity firms, but only for agencies that choose that authority.

Analysis: a venture that a large parent owns in whole or in majority is unlikely to be treated as separate from its parent when size is counted, because the parent holds the power to control. Ask early whether the proposed ownership split passes a program's tests.

## Tax treatment of a spin-off distribution

Some parents consider distributing the shares of a subsidiary to the parent's own shareholders. Section 355 of the Internal Revenue Code is the provision that describes when such a distribution is not taxed to the shareholders. The opening rule describes a distributing corporation that distributes stock or securities of a controlled corporation that it controlled immediately before the distribution. If the section's requirements are met, no gain or loss is recognized to the shareholder. The section requires that the distributing and the controlled corporation each be engaged immediately after the distribution in the active conduct of a trade or business, and it says the business must have been actively conducted throughout the five-year period ending on the date of the distribution. It also requires that the transaction was not used principally as a device for the distribution of the earnings and profits.

The sources do not cover the section's exceptions or how the Internal Revenue Service reads it. Analysis: a product only a few months old inside the parent is unlikely to have a long business history, which is a reason to ask a tax adviser early whether a new product line can be the business the section describes.

## Separating data and security

A spinout shares systems with its parent on day one. The National Institute of Standards and Technology describes its Cybersecurity Framework as helping organizations to better understand and improve their management of cybersecurity risk, and as being for industry, government and organizations generally. The page is general, and it does not say how to divide systems between a parent and a venture. Analysis: a venture that plans to sell on its own will be asked who can reach its customer data, and for a venture still on its parent's accounts and cloud environment the honest answer is that the parent's staff can.
## The routes side by side

| Route | Who owns what the team builds | Who can hold shares | What the parent can still decide |
|---|---|---|---|
| A team inside the parent | The parent, for employee work; patents need an assignment or obligation | Only the parent's own owners | Everything, through its own governance |
| A separate company owned by the parent | The new company, after written transfers | The parent alone | Its board and anything the certificate or an agreement reserves |
| A spinout with outside investors | The new company, with the parent's licenses and transfers defined | Investors, founders, employees and the parent | Only what its shares, seats and contracts give it |
| A license of the technology | The parent keeps title; the licensee builds | The licensee's owners | The terms of the license and nothing more |

Analysis: moving down the table, the venture gains independence and the parent loses direct control, and each step adds documents to be drafted before the people and the code move.

## Questions to ask before starting

**Does the venture need to sell to buyers the parent does not serve?** If not, the first route may be enough.

**Who wrote the code, and under which agreement?** List employees, contractors and any open source or third-party code, and find the paper that says who owns each.

**Which inventions might be patentable, and who is the inventor of each?** Check that an assignment or an obligation to assign exists before outside investors arrive.

**How will people be paid, and in what?** Ask counsel which securities rule covers any shares, how grant, vesting and sale are taxed, and whether any deferred pay falls within section 409A.

**What will each owner be able to block?** Write down the decisions that need the parent's consent and those that need anyone else's.

**Will the parent's size or ownership count against the venture?** Ask before applying to any program that counts affiliates.

**What will be separated, and when?** List systems, accounts, vendors and data, and the service agreement that covers the time they are shared.

## What this brief cannot tell you

This brief cannot tell you which route to take. That depends on facts about your company that no general document can know. It names no company, describes no particular arrangement and claims no corporate client. It describes no result from any venture.

It cannot tell you how a rule applies to your facts, because the agency pages are summaries and a rule's exceptions are mostly outside the evidence. It cannot tell you about state law beyond the Delaware director provisions, or about other countries, and those rules can change the answer. It cannot tell you the current text of any rule, because the evidence was read on a single day. It cannot tell you how often any route works, how long a separation takes or what it costs, because the evidence set contains no outcome data.

WE makes no promise of funding, and nothing in this brief is an offer, a recommendation or a promise of any outcome for any venture.
