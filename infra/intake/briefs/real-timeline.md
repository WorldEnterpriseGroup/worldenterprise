# The real timeline: how long a new business takes

This brief sets out what public United States data says about the time it takes to turn a new business into a lasting one. It is written for a founder who is deciding how to plan the first years of a company: how long to budget for, which milestones come first, and when the odds start to move in the company's favor. It is general information, current as of October 14, 2026. The figures describe all new U.S. businesses, not the results of any one founder, program or firm, and they are not a forecast for any single company.

## The question this brief answers

Most founders start with a date in mind. The product will be ready by spring, the first customers will sign by summer, and by the end of the year the company will be on its feet. The date is rarely chosen from evidence. It comes from enthusiasm, from the length of a savings account, or from the length of a program the founder has heard of.

The question here is simpler and harder: when public statistics follow large numbers of new businesses from the day they are registered, how long do those businesses take to hire, to survive and to become established? The answer matters because the length of the plan decides almost everything else. It decides how much money the company needs before it earns its own, how the founder lives in the meantime, and which decisions can wait.

A plan that needs the company to be established within a year is a plan built on a clock that public data does not support.

This brief does not say how long any particular company will take. It reports what the national series show, separates those reports from the analysis that follows each section, and ends with a list of what the data cannot tell a founder.

## About the evidence

The brief draws on seven documents, each retrieved and read on October 14, 2026. They come from three federal sources and one academic survey.

The U.S. Census Bureau publishes the Business Formation Statistics, a monthly series built from applications for an Employer Identification Number, the federal tax number a business requests from the Internal Revenue Service. The series counts the applications and then follows them, using payroll records, to see how many become businesses with employees and how long that takes. The brief uses the monthly data file and the Census Bureau's own page of definitions.

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes the Business Employment Dynamics program. Its Table 7 follows every private-sector establishment that opened in a given year, from the year to March 1994 onward, and reports how many are still operating each later year. The brief uses three versions of the table: all private industries, the Information sector (which includes software publishing) and the Professional, Scientific, and Technical Services sector.

The Office of Advocacy of the U.S. Small Business Administration publishes an annual set of answers to common questions about small business. The 2026 edition summarizes the survival rates of new employer establishments from 1994 to 2022.

The Global Entrepreneurship Monitor is an international survey of adults run by a consortium of universities; Babson College leads the United States team. The brief uses Babson's coverage of the 2025/2026 United States report for one definition only.

Three of the figures below are derived: they are sums or averages of the monthly Census values, computed for this brief and labelled as such. The Census Bureau revises the series every year, so a derived figure can move when the next revision is published. Every other figure is printed by its source.

## How many businesses start

Starting a business in the United States has rarely been more common. The Census Bureau counted 5,671,836 business applications across the twelve months of 2025, the highest year since the series began (derived: the sum of the monthly values that are not seasonally adjusted). For comparison, the same calculation gives 2,485,100 applications in 2005 and 3,498,990 in 2019. The count jumped to 4,356,498 in 2020 and to 5,390,816 in 2021, and it has stayed above five million in every year since.

An application is not a company with customers, and it is not an employer. The Census Bureau's definitions are careful on this point. The series counts requests for a tax number, and many of them belong to sole proprietors, to holding entities, or to ideas that never go further. The definitions page describes a separate series, high-propensity applications, for the subset whose characteristics suggest they are likely to become businesses with payroll.

Analysis. The record count says that many people are trying. It does not say that starting has become easier, and it says nothing about how long the attempts last. For a founder, the useful reading is that the first step, registering the company, is the shortest one by far. Everything this brief describes happens after it.

## From application to first payroll

The Census Bureau follows each month's applications forward to see which ones become employer businesses. Its definitions describe the measure BF8Q as the number of employer businesses that originate from business applications within eight quarters from the month of application, and BF4Q as the same count within four quarters.

For the applications filed in 2021, the monthly values add up to 315,318 businesses that reached a first payroll within four quarters, about 5.8 percent of the 5,390,816 applications that year. Within eight quarters the total was 403,072, about 7.5 percent (both derived from the monthly values).

The Census Bureau also measures how long that step takes. DUR8Q is the average duration, in quarters, between the month of application and the quarter in which the business formed, counting only the applications that formed within eight quarters. Averaged across the twelve months of each year, it was 2.29 quarters for applications filed in 2021, against 1.48 quarters for applications filed in 2005 (derived).

Analysis. Two points follow. First, reaching payroll is a filter that most applications never pass, so a first employee is a real milestone and not a formality. Second, among the businesses that did pass it, the wait from paperwork to payroll has grown by most of a quarter since 2005, from about four and a half months to about seven. A founder who expects to hire in the first quarter after registering is planning faster than the recent national average.

## How long new businesses last

The Bureau of Labor Statistics follows establishments rather than applications. An establishment is a single physical location with employees, so these figures describe businesses that have already reached payroll. That makes them a demanding comparison: every business counted here has already passed the filter of the previous section.

For the establishments that opened in the year to March 2024, 77.9 percent were still operating a year later. About one in five had closed in its first year.

Following one group over a full decade shows the shape of the curve. Of the private-sector establishments that opened in the year to March 2015, these shares were still operating at each anniversary:

| Years since opening | Still operating |
|---|---|
| 1 | 79.6% |
| 2 | 69.1% |
| 3 | 61.4% |
| 4 | 55.4% |
| 5 | 50.2% |
| 6 | 46.4% |
| 7 | 43.6% |
| 8 | 40.3% |
| 9 | 37.4% |
| 10 | 34.7% |

Later groups follow a similar path. Of the establishments that opened in the year to March 2020, 51.4 percent were still operating five years later. The Small Business Administration's summary puts the average five-year survival rate across the groups from 1994 to 2022 at 49.2 percent, and the average ten-year rate at 33.9 percent.

Analysis. About half of new employer establishments are gone by year five, and about a third are still operating at year ten. Those are averages over millions of businesses of every kind, from restaurants to software firms, and they include businesses that closed for reasons that had nothing to do with failure, such as an owner's retirement. Still, the curve has a clear message about time. The losses are steepest in the first two years, and they continue at a slower pace for a long while after. A business that is still young at year three is normal.

## The odds improve with every year

The same table can be read another way. Instead of asking what share of the original group is left, it asks what share of the businesses that were open at the start of a year were still open at its end. For the establishments that opened in the year to March 2013, that share rose almost every year:

| Year | Survived the year |
|---|---|
| Year 1 | 79.6% |
| Year 2 | 86.6% |
| Year 3 | 89.0% |
| Year 4 | 90.1% |
| Year 5 | 91.5% |
| Year 6 | 92.2% |
| Year 7 | 91.7% |
| Year 8 | 93.2% |
| Year 9 | 94.4% |

The Small Business Administration reports the same pattern over longer spans. Of the establishments that reach five years, 69.5 percent also reach ten, and 76.1 percent of those that reach ten also reach fifteen.

Analysis. Each year a business survives, its odds of surviving the next year rise. The first year is the hardest one; by the ninth year, about 19 of every 20 surviving businesses make it through another. For planning, this argues for treating the early years as the period in which the company earns its later stability, and for measuring progress in years rather than in months. It also argues against judging a young company's prospects from its first-year struggles alone.

## Software and professional services

National averages mix many industries. Table 7 is also published for each sector, and two are closest to a software-enabled or expertise-led company.

The Information sector includes software publishing along with media, telecommunications and data processing, so it is not a measure of software companies alone. Of the Information establishments that opened in the year to March 2020, 45.7 percent were still operating five years later. Professional, Scientific, and Technical Services covers firms such as consultancies, design and engineering practices and computer systems design. For the same group, 50.8 percent were still operating at five years.

Over a full decade, the group that opened in the year to March 2015 tells a similar story. In Information, 30.0 percent were still operating at year ten; in professional and technical services, 34.3 percent; across all private industries, 34.7 percent.

Analysis. Software is not a shortcut through the national curve. The sectors closest to it show survival close to the all-industry average, and the Information sector a little below it. A founder should not assume that a low-cost product, a remote team or a digital channel exempts the company from the timeline that other new businesses face.

## When a business counts as established

The Global Entrepreneurship Monitor separates people who are starting or running a new business from those who own an established one. Babson College's coverage of the 2025/2026 United States report describes established businesses as those at least three and a half years old: 42 months.

The coverage also reports that early-stage activity remains near historic highs while the ownership of established businesses has continued to decline, and the researchers describe that gap as a concern about whether new businesses are lasting.

Analysis. A survey that tracks entrepreneurship in economies around the world does not count a business as established until it is three and a half years old. That definition is a useful yardstick for a founder's plan. Read alongside the survival tables, it puts the period in which a company is still forming at several years, not a few months.

## What this means for planning

The figures above lead to a few practical consequences. Each is analysis, not a finding of the sources.

Plan in years. The national series measure the formation of a business in quarters and years: more than two quarters on average from application to payroll for those that get there, half of employer establishments gone by year five, and established status at 42 months. A plan that runs out at twelve or eighteen months leaves the company short of the period in which the odds are worst.

Separate the milestones. Registering, hiring, surviving the first year and becoming established are different events, and the data shows long gaps between them. A plan is easier to manage when each milestone has its own budget and its own test of progress, rather than one date for everything.

Fund the long middle. The years between the first payroll and established status are where the survival curve keeps falling, slowly. The company needs enough money, revenue or patience to stay in the game through them, and the founder needs a way to live while it does.

Review on a fixed rhythm. Because the odds change year by year, a yearly review that compares the company with where the curve says a business of its age usually stands is more useful than a single deadline.

Expect to be early for a long time. A company in its third year is still young by every measure in this brief.

## What this brief cannot tell you

These are statistics about very large groups. They describe the average path of millions of new businesses and say nothing reliable about the path of one.

The survival tables count establishments, not companies. A firm with several locations appears several times, and a closed location is not always a closed company. A closure can also be an owner's choice to stop rather than a failure.

The tables cover businesses with employees. A company that never hires, or that works only with contractors, is not in the Bureau of Labor Statistics series at all.

The Census application counts include many applications that were never meant to become operating businesses, so the share that reaches payroll understates the share of serious attempts that do.

The derived figures depend on the current release of the Census series, which is revised every year.

None of these sources measures the effect of any program, adviser or consulting firm on a company's survival, and nothing here should be read as evidence that outside help changes the odds. The figures say how long the road usually is. They do not say who will reach the end of it.
