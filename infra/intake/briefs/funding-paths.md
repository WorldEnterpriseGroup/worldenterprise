# Funding paths for an early software company

This brief compares the main ways a small software company can pay for its early work, and it is organized around what each way asks of the founder in return. It is general information, current as of October 10, 2026. It is not legal, tax or investment advice, and every rule it describes should be checked against the current text with a qualified professional before a decision rests on it.

## The question this brief answers

A founder with a product idea and a small team eventually asks how the work will be paid for. The usual answers arrive in a single breath: savings, customers, a grant, a loan, an investor, a crowd. They are presented as one menu, as if choosing among them were a matter of taste.

They are not interchangeable. Each one attaches different conditions to the money. Some are legal, such as the rules about who may be offered a security and how the offer may be advertised. Some are financial, such as repayment on a schedule or a claim on future shares. A founder who compares only the amount of money on offer is comparing the smallest part of each path.

This brief therefore asks a different question of each path: what does it require of the company, what does it require of the founder personally, and what does it leave behind once the money is spent? A funding path is a bundle of obligations before it is a source of money, and the obligations outlast the cash.

## About the evidence

This brief draws on eight documents, each retrieved and read on the day it was written, October 10, 2026. They fall into three groups.

The first group is government material: the Securities and Exchange Commission pages on exempt offerings, on the private placement exemption known as Rule 506(b) and on Regulation Crowdfunding; the SBIR program site, which is coordinated by the Small Business Administration; the Small Business Administration page on its loan programs; and the text of section 1202 of the Internal Revenue Code, as published by Cornell Law School's Legal Information Institute. They describe rules and programs as their authors state them, not how a rule applies to a particular company.

The second group is the publications of two organizations that describe instruments they wrote or maintain: Y Combinator's page for the SAFE and the National Venture Capital Association's page of model legal documents. They are used only to describe what the instruments are and what they are for. They are not neutral about their own documents.

The third group is the analysis in this brief itself. Where a sentence is a judgment rather than a report of a source, it is marked as analysis or phrased as a consideration. The analysis is not drawn from a study of outcomes, and no statistic about how often any path succeeds appears in this brief, because none of the eight sources supplies one.

Two limits on the sources deserve mention at the start. Several of the pages are summaries for a general audience, and a summary leaves out exceptions that the underlying rule contains. All of the material describes United States federal rules. State securities rules, other countries' rules and the terms of any particular program or lender are outside the sources.

## How to read the paths

Each path below is described by the same four questions: who supplies the money and what that party gets, what the rules say about how the money may be raised or used, what the company owes afterward in cash, paperwork or control, and what the path tends to suit. The fourth is the part that is analysis rather than reporting.

A path is described by its mechanism, never by its likelihood.

## Path one: customers and the founder's own money

The simplest path needs no outside document at all. The company is paid by its customers, and the founders cover the gap from their own savings or from another source of income.

No source in the evidence set describes this path, so the description here is analysis. Revenue does not create a claim on the company's shares and does not impose a repayment schedule. The obligations that come with it are the ones that come with having customers: contracts, delivery, support and tax. A founder's own savings put the founder's personal finances at risk but create no outside party with rights over the company.

Analysis: this path suits a company whose product can reach a paying customer before the team runs out of personal runway, and it tends to suit a founder who wants to keep decisions entirely in-house. It suits less well a product that needs a long period of build time before anyone can pay for it, because in that case the founders are financing the entire interval alone.

Analysis: money from customers is also the evidence that other paths ask for, since an investor, a lender or a program evaluating a small company will look for signs that someone outside the team wants the product.

## Path two: government research awards

The SBIR and STTR programs fund technology research and development at small businesses through federal agencies. The SBIR program site describes the arrangement this way: each participating agency runs its own program within guidelines established by Congress, and the Small Business Administration coordinates the whole.

The site describes the funding as something that does not require giving up shares, and it frames the purpose as developing a technology and charting a path toward commercialization. That distinguishes an award from an equity investment, which is paid for with ownership.

The path has its own obligations, and the site names little of them. Analysis: because each agency administers its own program, a company should expect the topics, the schedules and the paperwork to differ by agency. Seeking an award is then not a single application to a single fund. It is a decision about which agency's stated needs match the company's work, followed by writing to that agency's terms.

Analysis: this path suits a company whose product rests on a technical question that a federal agency is likely to care about. Because the site describes the program as technology-focused funding, it probably suits an ordinary business-software idea less well. A founder considering it should read what each participating agency says it is looking for before spending time on an application, and should treat an award as a way to fund a defined piece of technical work rather than as general funding for the company.

## Path three: loans

A loan is the one path in which the money comes back to the lender on a schedule, whatever happens to the company. The Small Business Administration page on its loan programs describes three programs: 7(a) loans, 504 loans and microloans. It says that loans backed by the agency make it easier for small businesses to reach affordable funding, and that the loans are delivered through partner lenders.

The page also says that guaranteed loans generally have rates and fees comparable to those on loans without a guarantee. Analysis: that suggests the guarantee mainly reduces the lender's risk rather than lowering the borrower's cost, so a founder should not assume that a backed loan is cheaper than an ordinary one.

The obligations of a loan are the plainest in this brief: repayment with interest, and whatever security or personal assurances the lender requires. The page does not describe the conditions that a given lender will set, and those conditions are where most of the practical difference lies.

Analysis: a loan suits a company with predictable revenue against which payments can be scheduled, and it suits a founder who prefers fixed obligations to shared ownership. It suits a pre-revenue software company poorly, because repayment begins regardless of whether the product has found its market. If a founder is asked to guarantee a loan personally, the personal consequences of a default belong in the decision, and a lawyer should read the guarantee before it is signed.

## Path four: selling shares in a private placement

Selling shares to outside investors is a sale of securities, and the Securities and Exchange Commission's overview states the governing principle: every offer and sale of securities must either be registered under the Securities Act of 1933 or fall within an exemption. A young company almost never registers an offering. It relies on an exemption.

The SEC page on exempt offerings lists the exemptions it covers: Regulation D, including Rules 506(b), 506(c) and 504, along with the Form D notice; Regulation Crowdfunding; Regulation A; intrastate offerings; and offerings under employee benefit plans. For a startup selling shares to investors it knows, the one most often discussed is Rule 506(b).

The SEC's page on Rule 506(b) states two features that shape how a company may raise money under it. The first concerns advertising. The page says general solicitation of investors is incompatible with the private placement exemption, so a company cannot publicly advertise the sale. The second concerns who may buy. The page says an issuer can sell to an unlimited number of accredited investors, while sales to non-accredited investors are limited to no more than 35 in any 90-calendar-day period, and each of those buyers must have enough knowledge and experience in financial and business matters to evaluate the investment, alone or with a purchaser representative.

Analysis: a company relying on this exemption finds its investors through relationships, not through a public campaign. The exemption also carries a filing obligation. The SEC page on exempt offerings says an issuer must file a Form D notice no later than 15 days after the first sale of securities in an offering relying on Rule 506(b).

This brief does not describe Rule 506(c), which the SEC overview names, because the evidence set does not contain its conditions. A founder who wants to advertise an offering should read the SEC's material on that rule directly.

Analysis: the obligations of this path are lasting. Sold shares give the investor a claim on the company's ownership and, usually, rights in how it is run, set in documents that are negotiated.

Analysis: this path suits a company whose founders can reach accredited investors through existing relationships, and it suits a founder prepared to share ownership and some decisions. It is a poor fit for a founder with no existing relationships who expects an advertisement to substitute for them.

## Path five: convertible instruments such as the SAFE

Many early rounds do not sell shares immediately. Instead the investor gives money now in exchange for a right to receive shares later. The best-known form is the SAFE, short for simple agreement for future equity, which Y Combinator publishes as an open standard for startup fundraising.

Y Combinator's page describes the instrument as a short contract in which an investor provides funding to a startup now in return for a right to receive company stock later. The page states that the SAFE converts into shares automatically when the startup raises a priced round, and that it is not a debt and not a loan, with no interest and no maturity date. It lists three versions of the post-money SAFE for companies in the United States, plus an optional side letter: one that sets a ceiling on the price at which the SAFE converts, one that gives a reduced price at conversion, and one that does neither and is known as the most favored nation version.

Analysis: because the instrument defers the question of what the company is worth, it also defers the negotiation of that value, which is one reason founders find it attractive. The deferral has a cost. The investor holds a right that will turn into ownership at a later event, and the founder will not know how much ownership has been promised until that event arrives. Several instruments signed at different times can each convert on different terms, and the combined effect on the founders' share is not visible until a priced round forces the arithmetic.

Analysis: a founder using convertible instruments should keep a running record of every instrument signed and should model the conversion before signing the next one. A short document is not a reason to skip a lawyer's review, and a standard form is not a standard outcome, because the effect of the instrument depends on the version chosen and on what follows it.

## Path six: a priced equity round

In a priced round the company and its investors agree on a price per share, the investors buy shares at that price and the terms of the relationship are written into a set of financing documents. The National Venture Capital Association publishes a set of model legal documents for exactly this purpose and describes them as the industry-embraced model documents to be used in venture capital financings.

The page lists the documents in the financing set: a certificate of incorporation, a stock purchase agreement, an investors' rights agreement, a voting agreement, a right of first refusal and co-sale agreement, a management rights letter, an indemnification agreement and a model legal opinion. It says each document is intended to serve as a starting point only and should be tailored to the specific requirements of the parties.

Two points follow. The first is that the set of documents is the real content of a priced round. The price per share is a single line, while the rights of the investors, the voting arrangements and the restrictions on sale are spread across several agreements, and each of them is a binding agreement. The second is that a model is a starting point. A negotiated deal departs from it, and the departures are what a founder's lawyer is for.

Analysis: a priced round suits a company that has enough evidence of demand for an investor to be willing to fix a price, and it suits founders prepared for the governance that follows. It is a heavy instrument for a very early company, where a simpler instrument might serve.

## Path seven: crowdfunding to the public

Regulation Crowdfunding is the exemption that lets a company raise money from a large number of ordinary people. The SEC page describes it as enabling eligible companies to offer and sell securities through crowdfunding, and it says the rules permit a company to raise a maximum aggregate amount through crowdfunding offerings in a 12-month period.

Two features set it apart from the private placement. The first is the intermediary. The SEC page says the rules require all transactions under Regulation Crowdfunding to take place online through an SEC-registered intermediary, which is either a broker-dealer or a funding portal. A company cannot run the offering on its own site. The second is disclosure. The page says the rules require disclosure in filings with the Commission and to investors and the intermediary, and it limits how much individual non-accredited investors can invest across all crowdfunding offerings in a 12-month period.

The obligations are therefore public ones. The company discloses information to a broad audience and then has many small holders of its securities. The page does not describe the cost of the platform or of managing a large group of holders.

Analysis: this path suits a company with a product that ordinary customers care about, since the same people can become investors, and it suits a founder who is comfortable with public disclosure. It suits a business-to-business product with a narrow set of buyers much less well, because there is no natural crowd.

## A tax note on shares

One piece of tax law affects how investors and founders think about equity in a small company, and it deserves a plain description. Section 1202 of the Internal Revenue Code provides a partial exclusion of gain from the sale of qualified small business stock. As published by Cornell Law School, the section says a taxpayer other than a corporation may exclude part of the gain, that the stock must be acquired at its original issue, directly or through an underwriter, and that it must be held for a minimum period, which the text sets differently depending on when the stock was acquired.

The consequence for a founder is that how shares are issued matters later. Stock bought from the company when it is first issued is acquired at original issue. Stock bought later from another holder is not. This brief does not summarize any other condition of the section, including conditions on the company that issues the stock, and the section text should be read in full with an accountant, since it depends on facts that are set when the shares are issued.

## The paths side by side

| Path | Who supplies the money | What the company owes afterward | What the evidence set describes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Customers and savings | Customers and the founders | Ordinary business obligations | Nothing, so the description is analysis |
| Government research award | A participating federal agency | Work against the agency's terms | SBIR program site |
| Loan | A lender, often backed by an agency | Repayment with interest | SBA loan programs page |
| Private placement | Accredited investors the company reaches | Shares, investor rights, a Form D notice | SEC pages on exempt offerings and Rule 506(b) |
| Convertible instrument | An investor | A right to future shares | Y Combinator's SAFE page |
| Priced round | Investors in a negotiated round | Shares and the rights in the financing documents | NVCA model documents page |
| Crowdfunding | Many small investors through a registered intermediary | Public disclosure and many holders | SEC page on Regulation Crowdfunding |

Read down the third column and a pattern appears. Analysis: the paths that ask least of the company in structure, customers and awards, are the most constrained in what the money can be used for, while the paths most flexible about use, loans and shares, ask for repayment or ownership in return. No path is cheap in every dimension.

## Questions to ask before choosing

These questions test a path against the company in hand. They do not produce an answer.

**What would this path require of the company six months after the money arrives?** A loan requires a payment. An investor requires reporting and, in a priced round, a place in decisions. An award requires work against agreed terms. If the answer is a surprise, the path has not been understood.

**Who can legally be asked, and how?** The private placement and crowdfunding exemptions limit both the buyers and the way the offer may be made.

**What happens if the money runs out before the evidence arrives?** A loan keeps demanding payment. An investor may or may not provide more. A government award ends when its defined work ends. The answer determines how much margin the plan needs.

**Who will read the documents with the founder?** Every path involves a document that binds the company, and reading it with a lawyer before signing is cheaper than renegotiating afterward.

## What this brief cannot tell you

This brief cannot tell you which path to take. That depends on facts about your company, your team and your market that no general document can know.

It cannot tell you whether any of these paths will be available to you. A program, a lender or an investor decides for itself, and nothing in the sources suggests that any outcome is assured. WE makes no promise of funding, and nothing in this brief should be read as implying that a company that follows the steps it describes will raise money.

It cannot tell you the current terms of any program. Award amounts, loan conditions, filing thresholds and holding periods are the kind of detail that changes, and the evidence for this brief was read on a single day. Check the source before relying on a figure.

It cannot tell you how a rule applies to your facts. The SEC pages are summaries for a general audience. The tax section is a description of one provision, not an analysis of whether your shares would qualify. The SBIR and SBA pages describe programs, not eligibility decisions.

It cannot tell you about other jurisdictions. Everything here is about United States federal rules. State securities requirements, which also apply to many offerings, are not covered, and neither are the rules of any other country.

It cannot tell you how often any path works. The evidence set contains no outcome data, and this brief offers none.

Finally, it is analysis in places, and the analysis can be wrong. The sections above mark where a sentence is a judgment. A founder should treat those sentences as questions to test, not as findings.
