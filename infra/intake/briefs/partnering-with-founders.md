# Partnering with early-stage founders: structures, expectations and written terms

This brief is for an organization that is about to work with a founder of an early-stage software company: a service provider, a platform vendor, an ecosystem or community program, an agency or a supplier. It describes how such arrangements are usually built, which questions each one raises and what a public source says about the legal rules underneath. It is general information, current as of October 11, 2026. It is not legal, tax or investment advice, and every rule it mentions should be checked against the current text with a qualified professional before a decision rests on it.

## The question this brief answers

Partnerships with early-stage founders usually begin with goodwill and a short message. A provider offers a tool or a place in a program, and the founder says yes because the offer is useful and the company is small. Later the two sides discover that they understood the arrangement differently: who owns the code that was written together, whether the founder may use the same tool with a competitor of the partner, what happens to customer data that passed through both companies, and who may end the arrangement.

Those disputes are rarely about bad faith. They come from an arrangement that was never turned into words. A working arrangement is a set of promises about who owns the work, who may use it and who may stop, and the promises that matter most are written before the work begins.

This brief therefore asks the partner's question in the founder's terms. For each common structure it asks what the structure requires of the partner, what it requires of the founder and what a written agreement has to say so that neither side is surprised. It names no company, describes no particular arrangement and does not predict how any arrangement will turn out.

## How to read the arrangements

The brief groups arrangements by what they do, not by what they are called, because the same word covers very different structures. A reader should ignore the label and ask four things of any arrangement: what each side contributes, what each side receives, what the arrangement produces and who owns it, and how it ends. The sections that follow take the common structures in turn and then the legal questions that cut across them. The questions come last, in a single list, so that they can be taken into a meeting.

## Kinds of arrangement

Six structures account for most of what founders are offered. The descriptions below are analysis, not a finding from a source; they are a vocabulary for the later sections.

**Referral or resale.** One side sends customers to the other, or sells the other side's product under its own terms. The central questions are who contracts with the customer and who holds the customer relationship if the arrangement ends.

**Integration or listing.** The founder's product connects to the partner's platform, or appears in a directory the partner runs. The central questions are what interface the founder may rely on, who may change it and what data crosses it.

**Co-development.** The two sides build something together. This structure has the hardest ownership questions, because both sides contribute to the same result.

**Pilot or design partnership.** The partner uses an early version of the founder's product and gives feedback. The questions are what the partner may do with the pilot version and whether feedback becomes the partner's property.

**Supplier terms.** The partner provides a service to the founder, such as hosting or a software license, on terms the partner usually wrote. The questions are about continuity, data, price changes and what happens to the founder's work if the supplier stops.

**Program participation.** An ecosystem or community program offers a founder access to resources or events under program terms the founder accepts. The questions are about what the founder gives up to take part, such as the right to share information or to deal with competing programs.

A single arrangement often mixes these, and the terms may conflict.

## Who owns what the work produces

Ownership is the first thing a written agreement settles, and the default rules do not always favor the party who expects them to.

Under United States copyright law, the starting point is the author. Section 201 of the Copyright Act says, "Copyright in a work protected under this title vests initially in the author or authors of the work." A partner that pays for software to be written, or whose staff contributes to it, does not become the owner by paying or by contributing ideas. Ownership follows authorship unless a rule or an agreement moves it.

The first rule that moves it is the doctrine of the work made for hire. The statute says that in that case the employer or other person for whom the work was prepared "is considered the author." Two situations qualify. The definition in section 101 covers "a work prepared by an employee within the scope of his or her employment," and it covers certain specially commissioned works, which qualify only "if the parties expressly agree in a written instrument signed by them that the work shall be considered a work made for hire." The commissioned category is limited to the kinds of work the section lists, such as contributions to a collective work, translations and compilations. The nine categories do not name software as such, so a founder and a partner who rely on a commissioned-work clause for ordinary code are relying on a rule whose fit is a question for counsel.

The second way ownership moves is a transfer. Section 201 says that the ownership of a copyright "may be transferred in whole or in part by any means of conveyance," and a written agreement is the ordinary instrument. A transfer is different from a license. A transfer moves the ownership, and a license leaves it where it was and permits a use. A partner that needs only to use the founder's software can ask for a license, and a founder who signs an assignment when a license was intended has given away more than the arrangement needed. That sentence is analysis: the statute describes the instrument, and the choice between the two is a negotiating position.

Finally there is the case where the two sides write the code together. The definition of a joint work in section 101 is "a work prepared by two or more authors with the intention that their contributions be merged" into "inseparable or interdependent parts of a unitary whole." Section 201 adds that "The authors of a joint work are coowners of copyright in the work." Co-owners are not a tidy outcome for a company that wants to raise money or be acquired later, because a future buyer will ask who else owns the code. The practical reading, offered as analysis, is that a co-development agreement should say in advance, in a signed document, whether the result is owned by one side with a license to the other or owned jointly.

## Patents and joint ownership

Patent law handles ownership differently from copyright, and the default for joint owners is the part that surprises people.

Section 261 of the Patent Act treats a patent like property: "Subject to the provisions of this title, patents shall have the attributes of personal property." It also says that "Applications for patent, patents, or any interest therein, shall be assignable in law by an instrument in writing." An assignment of a patent right is therefore a written instrument, and the same section sets rules on recording an assignment with the Patent and Trademark Office. Under that section, an assignment is void against a later purchaser or mortgagee who paid valuable consideration without notice, unless it is recorded no later than three months after its date or before the later purchase or mortgage. A founder who hands a partner a patent right, or a partner who receives one, should ask whether the transfer has been recorded.

Section 262 covers joint owners. The text begins "In the absence of any agreement to the contrary," and then says that each of the joint owners of a patent may make, use, offer to sell or sell the patented invention "without the consent of and without accounting to the other owners." In plain terms, unless the co-owners agree otherwise, each one may practice the invention in the United States without asking the others and without sharing what it earns. A founder who jointly owns a patent with a partner could find the partner licensing or practicing it in competition with the founder, and nothing in the statute stops that.

The consideration that follows is analysis. Joint ownership of a patent is a default that a written agreement can change, and the questions to settle in writing are who may license, who may sue and who pays for filing and upkeep.

## Open-source obligations

Many early-stage products are built on open-source components, and a partner who integrates with, hosts or resells such a product inherits questions about those components.

The Open Source Initiative says, in the opening line of its definition, "Open source doesn’t just mean access to the source code." The definition lists criteria that a license must meet. Among them, the license must allow modifications and derived works, and the rights attached to a program must apply to everyone to whom the program is redistributed, without the need for an additional license. Those criteria describe what a license must permit to count as open source. They do not describe the conditions that individual licenses attach, and those conditions differ.

For a partner the relevant point, which is analysis, is that a license can attach obligations to redistribution: notices to keep, source code to offer or terms to pass along. A partner that bundles a founder's product into its own offering is redistributing it, and may take on those obligations without having read the licenses. A sensible written arrangement asks for a list of open-source components and their licenses and says who keeps it current. The definition does not require such a list.

## Data and security expectations

Most arrangements between a founder and a larger organization move data, and most disputes about data come from a contract that says too little about it.

The European Union's General Data Protection Regulation is the clearest public statement of the idea. It distinguishes a controller from a processor that works for it. Article 28 says that "Processing by a processor shall be governed by a contract or other legal act under Union or Member State law," and among the terms the contract must set is that the processor "processes the personal data only on documented instructions from the controller." The regulation applies where its own scope rules say it does, and whether a given founder or partner falls within them is a question for counsel. It is useful here as an example of a rule that makes the contract itself the object of regulation: the written terms are not a courtesy, they are what the law looks at.

On the security side, many larger organizations send suppliers a questionnaire before they agree to anything. The National Institute of Standards and Technology publishes a framework for secure software development, SP 800-218, which describes itself as "a core set of high-level secure software development practices that can be integrated into each SDLC implementation." The abstract also says that "software purchasers and consumers can also use it to foster communications with suppliers in acquisition processes." This brief reads the framework as guidance and not as a certification, because its abstract describes practices and does not describe a certificate. It gives a partner and a founder a shared vocabulary for a conversation about practices.

The consideration for an early-stage founder, offered as analysis, is that a security questionnaire written for a large supplier may ask for things a small team has not built yet. A candid description of what exists and what is planned is more useful to both sides than a form filled in with hopes. A written arrangement should say what data crosses the boundary, who may see it, where it is kept, how it is deleted at the end and who is told if something goes wrong.

## How the people are classified

A partner that places its own people with a founder's company, or a founder who hires help through a partner, runs into the question of whether a worker is an employee or an independent contractor. The label in the contract does not settle it.

The Internal Revenue Service sorts the evidence into three categories: behavioral control, financial control and the type of relationship. Its questions include "Are the business aspects of the worker’s job controlled by the payer?" and, for the third category, whether there are written contracts or employee-type benefits. It also says, "The keys are to look at the entire relationship and consider the extent of the right to direct and control the worker." The page describes how that agency looks at the question.

The Department of Labor approaches the same subject under a different law, the Fair Labor Standards Act. Its page says, "Misclassification occurs when an employer treats a worker who is an employee under the FLSA as an independent contractor." The two pages come from agencies that administer different laws, and the sources do not say how a worker whose status is reviewed under both would be classified.

The consideration for a partnership is that an arrangement in which one organization directs the daily work of another organization's people can create questions about who the employer is, whatever the agreement is called. The structure to check is the actual direction of the work, not the heading of the contract. Where people move between organizations, the agreement should say who supervises, who pays and who is responsible for the legal obligations of an employer.

## When a partner takes shares in place of fees

Some partners offer to be paid in shares or rights to buy shares of the founder's company instead of fees, or alongside them. That turns a service agreement into a sale of securities, and the securities rules apply to the founder's company.

The SEC's page on Rule 506(b) describes one common route. Under it an issuer can sell to an unlimited number of accredited investors, and securities "may not be sold to more than 35 non-accredited investors in any 90-calendar day period." The offering may involve "no general solicitation or advertising to market the securities." The page says the issuer must file a notice on Form D no later than 15 days after the first sale of securities. Those are the page's statements about one exemption. State securities requirements also apply to many offerings, and the page does not cover them.

What follows is analysis. A partner who accepts shares for services becomes a holder of the company's securities, with consequences for the company's ownership records and its later fundraising. These are questions for counsel on both sides, and this brief takes no position on whether the choice is wise.

## Term, termination and restrictions on dealing with others

The last group of terms is the one most likely to be skimmed and most likely to matter. The sources in this brief do not state rules about these terms, so what follows is a list of questions, not a report of law.

On duration, the question is how long the arrangement runs, whether it renews by itself and what notice either side must give to end it.

On exit, the question is what each side keeps. The founder should ask whether the product, data and customer relationships come back intact. The partner should ask whether it may keep using the work it has paid for.

On restrictions, the question is whether either side has agreed not to deal with others. A clause that seems modest during a pilot can become a serious constraint once the company grows. State law on such restrictions varies, and the sources here do not address it.

On changes, the question is who may alter the terms and how the other side is told. A founder who builds a product on top of a platform's terms is exposed if the platform may change them.

## Questions to ask before signing

The questions are written for either side. Each can be asked by a founder or by a partner, and each is a prompt for a conversation with counsel, not a legal test.

**Who owns the work, and where is that written?** If the answer is only an assumption, the agreement has not yet settled it.

**Is this a license or a transfer?** A partner that needs only to use something does not necessarily need to own it.

**If the two organizations write the code together, who owns the result?** An answer of "jointly" should be followed by what each co-owner may do without the other.

**What open-source components are involved, and what do their licenses require?**

**What data crosses the boundary, and what does the contract say about it?**

**Who directs the people?** If one organization directs the daily work of the other's staff, the classification question is live whatever the heading says.

**Is anyone taking shares, and under what rule?**

**How does it end?** Duration, notice, the handing back of materials and any restriction on dealing with others should be written before they are needed.

**Who reads the documents with each side?** Reading a binding document with counsel before signing is cheaper than renegotiating afterward.

## What this brief cannot tell you

This brief is general information. It does not recommend a structure to any reader, and it cannot tell you which arrangement suits your organization or a founder, because that depends on facts about both sides that no general document can know.

It names no company and describes no particular arrangement. Nothing in it refers to a real agreement, a real founder or a real outcome, and nothing in it offers an introduction, a partnership or funding. It should not be read as implying that any organization that follows the steps it describes will form a partnership or raise money.

It cannot tell you how a rule applies to your facts. The statutes are quoted as the Legal Information Institute publishes them, and the agency pages are summaries for a general audience. How a court would treat a particular clause, worker or issuance of shares is a question for counsel.

It cannot tell you about other legal systems. Apart from one European regulation, everything here is United States federal law. State law, which governs much of contract, employment and securities practice, is not covered.

It cannot tell you the current state of the rules, because the evidence was read on one day. It cannot tell you how often any structure works, because the evidence set contains no outcome data. And it is analysis in places, which can be wrong: a reader should treat the sentences marked as judgments as questions to test, not as findings.
