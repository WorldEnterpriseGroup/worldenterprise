# A pre-seed evidence pack: what to gather before an investor conversation

This brief describes a set of records a founder can assemble before talking to an outside investor about a first round. It is general information, current as of October 11, 2026. It is not legal, tax or investment advice, and every rule it describes should be read in its current text, with counsel, before a decision rests on it. Nothing in it says or implies that assembling these records leads to an investment.

## The question this brief answers

Founders preparing for a first conversation with an investor tend to hear the same advice in two forms. One form is a list of documents to have ready. The other is a warning that the documents will be asked for at the worst possible moment. Neither explains why those documents, or what a reader is meant to learn from each.

This brief takes the question from the other direction. It asks what each kind of record shows about a young company, what a founder can check in a public source about the legal setting around it, and what no record can show. It does not describe what any particular investor will ask for, because no source in its evidence set does, and requests vary from one investor to the next.

A pack of evidence organizes what is already true about a company; it does not make anything true, and it does not decide what any investor will do.

## About the evidence

This brief draws on nine documents, each retrieved and read on October 11, 2026. They fall into three groups.

The first group is government and statute. The Securities and Exchange Commission publishes a page on exempt offerings and a page on the private placement exemption known as Rule 506(b). The Small Business Administration describes what a business plan contains and why market research matters. Cornell Law School's Legal Information Institute publishes the text of three federal statutes: the copyright definitions, the rule on how copyright ownership is transferred and the rule on how patents are assigned.

The second group is the publications of two organizations that describe instruments they wrote or maintain. Y Combinator publishes the SAFE, and the National Venture Capital Association publishes model financing documents. They are used here only to describe what those instruments are. They are not neutral about their own documents.

The third group is analysis. Where a sentence is a judgment and not a report of a source, it is marked as analysis or phrased as a consideration. No source in the set describes what investors ask for before a first round, how often a pack is read or what it changes, so this brief contains no statistic about investors or outcomes, and it makes no claim about what investors prefer.

Two limits apply throughout. Most of the sources are summaries written for a general audience, and a summary omits exceptions that the underlying rule contains. And all of the legal material is United States federal law. State law, other countries' law and the terms of any particular investor or instrument are outside it.

## What an evidence pack is for

Analysis: an evidence pack has two readers, and they want different things.

The first reader is an investor, who is deciding whether to spend further time on a company. For that reader the pack answers a small number of questions quickly: who owns the company, who built the product and whether the company owns what they built, what promises have already been made to other investors, what the company says it will do, and what customers or the market have told it so far.

The second reader is the founder. Assembling the pack is a way of finding out whether the company can answer those questions. A founder who discovers that a former contractor wrote half the code under no written agreement is better off learning it while writing the pack than while an investor's lawyer reads it.

For both readers, the value of a pack lies in its being checkable. A statement that can be tied to a signed document or a dated record is a different kind of statement from one that cannot.

Analysis: a pack is also a way of choosing what not to say. A document that overstates a company's position will be tested against the records behind it, and the gap is read as information about the founders.

## Who owns the company

The first category is the plainest. A reader wants to know who holds the company's shares, what rights come with them and what has been promised but not yet issued.

Analysis: a useful record here has four parts.

- A current list of every holder, with what each holds and when it was issued.
- The documents that created those holdings, signed and dated.
- Any promise of future shares, whether to a founder, an employee, an adviser or an earlier backer.
- The founders' own agreements with the company and with each other, including what happens if one leaves.

The point of the list is that it matches the documents. A list that shows a holding with no document behind it, or a document with no entry on the list, is the kind of mismatch that a reader finds in minutes.

This brief does not describe how a company is formed or how its shares are issued, which differ by state and by entity type. It treats those facts as inputs. Counsel can say whether the company's records are complete and whether any holding needs to be repaired.

## Who owns the work

The second category is what the company actually owns. For a software company this means mostly code, designs and written material, and sometimes inventions.

The rules for who owns a piece of work are in statute, and the statutes put the form of the agreement in front. On copyright, the definition section of the federal code says that a work made for hire is "a work prepared by an employee within the scope of his or her employment." For a work made by someone who is not an employee, it describes a narrower set of categories, and in those cases the parties must "expressly agree in a written instrument signed by them" that the work is made for hire.

A separate section covers transfers. It says, "A transfer of copyright ownership, other than by operation of law, is not valid unless an instrument of conveyance" or a note or memorandum of the transfer is in writing and signed by the owner of the rights conveyed or the owner's authorized agent. In substance, a transfer that does not happen by operation of law needs a signed writing.

Patents follow a similar pattern. The patent statute says, "Applications for patent, patents, or any interest therein, shall be assignable in law by an instrument in writing." The statute also provides that an assignment may be void against a later buyer or lender who gives value without notice unless it is recorded with the Patent and Trademark Office within three months of its date or before the later purchase. The details are in the statute, and a founder with a patent application should have counsel read them.

Analysis: three practical points follow from these provisions.

- An employee's work for the company is treated differently from a contractor's. A company that has used contractors, including friends who helped early, should check whether each of them signed something that moves ownership of their work to the company.
- A founder who wrote code before the company was formed owns that code personally unless something moved it to the company. The move is a document, and it should exist.
- Open-source components and third-party libraries carry their own licenses. They are not addressed by the sources here, but a record of what the product includes is part of knowing what the company owns.

These are questions for counsel. The sources describe what the statutes say. They do not say how a statute applies to a particular set of facts.

## The instruments already signed

The third category is every instrument the company has already signed with someone who put money in or promised to. A reader needs to know what it says, because it affects what a later investor receives.

The SAFE is the instrument most often seen at an early stage, and its publisher describes it plainly. Y Combinator says that "A SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity) is a short contract an investor signs to fund your startup now." It also says that "It converts into shares automatically when your startup raises a priced round." The page lists versions of the document that differ in the terms that decide how the conversion is calculated, and an optional side letter that holds a separate right to take part in later rounds. A company that has signed several SAFEs on different versions has a conversion calculation to understand, and a reader will want to see it worked out.

For a priced round, the National Venture Capital Association publishes model documents. It says that "The NVCA Model Legal Documents serve as the industry-embraced model documents to be used in venture capital financings." The page lists a certificate of incorporation, a stock purchase agreement, an investors' rights agreement, a voting agreement and a right of first refusal and co-sale agreement, among others. The page is a catalog, and it does not say which documents any particular financing will use.

Analysis: the useful form of this record is a one-page summary of each instrument, with the date, the parties and the terms that matter, followed by the signed document. The summary is for the reader's convenience. The document governs.

Both publishers describe their own products. This brief uses them to say what the instruments are and does not endorse either, and it does not say that either is the right choice for a given company.

## How the raise may be talked about in public

The fourth category is not a document. It is a set of limits on how a company may describe an offering of its securities, and a pack can be part of how a company breaks them.

The Securities and Exchange Commission says that "Every offer and sale of securities must either be registered under the Securities Act of 1933" or fall within an exemption. The agency's page also says that "The SEC does not charge any filing fee for a Form D notice or amendment." It also states that a Form D notice is required for all Regulation D offerings.

On the private placement exemption known as Rule 506(b), the Commission's page says there can be "no general solicitation or advertising to market the securities." It says that an issuer relying on the rule can sell to an unlimited number of accredited investors, and that when non-accredited investors take part the issuer must give them disclosure documents.

Analysis: for a founder assembling a pack, the lesson is about distribution. A pack sent to a chosen person in a conversation is a different act from a deck posted on a public website with an invitation to invest. Whether a particular act is a general solicitation is a legal question that depends on the facts, and this brief does not answer it. A founder who is unsure should ask counsel before publishing anything that describes an offering, and should keep a record of who was sent what.

The pages are summaries. They do not cover state securities laws, which also apply, and they do not say which exemption suits a given company.

## The plan and what it has to support

The fifth category is what the company says it will do. The Small Business Administration describes the parts of a business plan in short sentences. The executive summary should "Briefly tell your reader what your company is and why it will be successful." The marketing and sales section should "describe how you’ll attract and retain customers." The funding request should "clearly explain how much funding you’ll need over the next five years and what you’ll use it for" and should be supplemented with financial projections. The agency adds that a business plan is a roadmap for the owner and that traditional plans are commonly requested by lenders and investors.

The page is guidance for small businesses in general. It describes a traditional plan, and a pre-seed company may present a shorter document, so the sections are a checklist of questions and not a required format.

Analysis: what matters for a pack is the relationship between the plan and the records. Each statement in a plan should be one the company can support. If the plan says customers are asking for a feature, there should be a record of customers asking. If it says the product is built, there should be a version a reader can see. A plan that outruns its records invites the reader to trust the records instead, and that is a poor trade for the plan.

Projections are the weakest part of an early plan, and the sources do not tell a founder how to make them. A defensible practice is to show the assumptions beside each figure, so that a reader can see what would have to be true.

## Evidence about customers and the market

The sixth category is what the outside world has said to the company. The Small Business Administration says that "Market research helps you find customers for your business" and that "Competitive analysis helps you learn from businesses competing for your potential customers." It says that a business can gather demographic information and can survey or interview potential buyers directly for specific insights.

Analysis: a few kinds of evidence recur at this stage, and they differ in how much they can show.

- **Conversations with potential customers.** Dated notes, with the person's role and what they said, show what the company has learned. They show that the founders asked. They do not show that anyone will buy.
- **Letters of interest or pilots.** A signed pilot shows more than a note, because it records what the customer agreed to do. Its value depends on its terms.
- **Paying customers.** An invoice and a payment are the strongest records, because they cannot be written for the occasion. A reader will ask who the customers are, how they found the company and whether they are still customers.
- **Use of the product.** Records of use show whether people who start continue. The reader will ask how the figures were collected and whether they include the founders' own accounts.

None of these is required by a source. They are categories that a founder can sort the company's own records into, and the honest label for each is what it shows and what it does not. A conversation record is not a sales record, and a pack that mixes the two weakens both.

## Keeping the pack current

A pack decays. Shares are issued, contracts are signed, a contractor leaves and the plan changes. A reader who finds a stale pack learns that the company does not keep its records.

Analysis: three habits keep a pack useful.

- One person owns it, and it has a date at the top that changes when the contents change.
- A change to the company's ownership or its instruments is entered the day it happens, not when the next conversation is scheduled.
- The pack is kept in one place with access limited to the people who need it, and the company records who has seen which version.

The last habit has a legal side and a practical one. The legal side is the point of the previous section about public statements. The practical side is that a company which knows who holds a copy can correct it.

## Questions to ask before you share the pack

These questions are for the founders and, where they involve a document or a rule, for counsel.

**Does every holding on the ownership list have a signed document, and does every document have a holding?** A gap in either direction should be repaired before a reader finds it.

**For each person who built part of the product, is there a signed writing that moves their work to the company?** The statutes put the writing first.

**What does each signed instrument say, and what would happen to each of them in a priced round?** A reader will want the calculation shown.

**Who is the pack being sent to, and did the company choose that person?** The answer bears on the limits on public offering described above.

**Can the company support every sentence of its plan with a record?** A sentence that cannot be supported should be removed or marked as an assumption.

**What does the company not know?** A short list of open questions, stated plainly, reads better than a pack that appears to have no gaps.

## What this brief cannot tell you

This brief cannot tell you what an investor will ask for. It describes categories of records and what each shows, and no source in its evidence set reports what investors request or how they weigh it.

It cannot tell you whether assembling a pack will lead to a meeting, an offer or an investment. It does not, and nothing in it should be read as suggesting that any record, however complete, produces an investment. An investor decides for itself, and WE makes no promise of funding.

It cannot tell you whether your records are in order. The statutes describe what the law requires for a transfer of ownership, and none of the pages says how the law applies to your facts. A lawyer who reads your own documents can.

It cannot tell you which instrument to sign or what terms to accept. The two publishers describe their own documents, and the brief does not recommend either.

It cannot tell you whether a particular communication is a general solicitation, or which exemption suits your company. The Commission's pages are summaries, and state securities laws are outside the sources.

It cannot tell you about other jurisdictions. Everything here concerns United States federal rules.

It does not describe the clients, results or terms of WE.

Finally, it contains analysis, and the analysis can be wrong. The sections above mark where a sentence is a judgment. Treat those sentences as questions to test with counsel and with the people who will read the pack.
