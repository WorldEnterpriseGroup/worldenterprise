# Company setup for a first-time founder

This brief walks through the legal and administrative choices a founder makes when turning a project into a company, and it is organized around the order in which they tend to arise. It is general information, current as of October 10, 2026. It is not legal, tax or investment advice, and every rule it describes should be checked against the current text with a qualified professional before a decision rests on it.

## The question this brief answers

A founder with a working prototype and a few collaborators reaches a point where informal arrangements stop being enough. Someone has to sign a contract with a customer. Someone has to be paid. Someone has to own the code. The founders have to say, in writing, who holds what share of the thing they are building.

The usual advice arrives as a checklist: form an entity, get a tax number, sort out the shares, protect the name. A checklist treats each item as a form to be completed. This brief treats each item as a decision with consequences that the form does not show, and it asks of each one a plain question: what does this choice settle, what does it leave open, and what does it cost to change later?

The shortest statement of the answer is this. Setting up a company is a sequence of small decisions, and each one is cheap to make early and expensive to undo later. The sections below name the decisions, report what public sources say about each, and mark where the brief is giving its own judgment.

## About the evidence

This brief draws on nine documents, each retrieved and read on the day it was written, October 10, 2026. They fall into three groups.

The first group is federal agency guidance: two Internal Revenue Service pages on limited liability companies and on S corporations; the Internal Revenue Service page on applying for an employer identification number; the Small Business Administration guide to choosing a business structure; the U.S. Copyright Office page on what copyright is; and the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office page on what a trademark is. These pages are written for a general audience and describe the rule in outline.

The second group is primary legal text as published by Cornell Law School's Legal Information Institute: section 101 of the Copyright Act, which defines a work made for hire, and the Treasury regulation that sets out how the election under section 83(b) of the Internal Revenue Code is made. The regulation is the actual rule, not a summary of it. It is also the source in this brief most likely to have been amended, because the page is a snapshot of text that can change.

The third group is one state source: the Delaware Division of Corporations page on forming an entity. It describes what Delaware requires, and says nothing about any other state.

The analysis in this brief is its own. Where a sentence is a judgment rather than a report of a source, it is marked as analysis or phrased as a consideration. No statistic about how many founders choose any structure appears here, because none of the nine sources supplies one.

Two limits on the evidence deserve mention at the start. The sources describe United States federal rules and one state. And several are summaries, which leave out exceptions that the underlying rule contains.

## How to read the choices

Each choice below is described the same way: what a source says the mechanism is, what the choice settles, and what a founder should confirm with a professional. The parts that are analysis are labeled.

The order matters less than it seems, with one exception: a deadline that starts when something happens, such as the election about shares in the fourth choice.

## Choice one: the legal form of the company

The Small Business Administration guide compares the main structures a business can take. It describes a sole proprietorship as having no separate legal entity, so that the owner can be held personally liable for the debts and obligations of the business. It describes a partnership as two or more owners sharing a business, and says that in a general partnership the owners generally face unlimited personal liability.

The guide describes a limited liability company as combining features of corporations and partnerships, with liability protection and pass-through taxation. It says that limited liability companies protect owners from personal liability in most instances. The phrase "in most instances" is the guide's own, and it is worth reading closely. The protection is real, and it is not absolute.

The guide describes a corporation as a separate legal entity that offers the strongest protection to its owners from personal liability, and that pays income tax on its own profits, so that profits can be taxed again when they are paid out as dividends. It describes an S corporation as a special corporation designed to avoid that double taxation by passing income through to owners, with eligibility limits and a filing with the IRS.

What this choice settles is who is responsible when something goes wrong, and how the profits are reported. Analysis: for a company that expects to hire, sign customer contracts and take on obligations in its own name, the separate entity is the point. A sole proprietorship gives the least separation between the founder and the business. The guide itself says its comparison is only a guideline and recommends confirming the choice with a business tax specialist.

The guide does not say which of these forms suits a company that intends to sell shares to outside investors, and this brief does not claim to answer that. Analysis: a founder who expects to raise money should ask a lawyer which form investors will expect before choosing, because a form chosen early can be changed only by a conversion, with paperwork and sometimes tax consequences.

## Choice two: where to form it

A company is formed under the law of a particular state, and the founders choose which. The Delaware Division of Corporations page describes what Delaware requires of an entity formed there. It says that Delaware law requires that every business entity have and maintain a registered agent in the State of Delaware, and that the agent must have a physical street address in the state. A business physically located in Delaware may act as its own agent.

The page also describes the continuing filings. All corporations incorporated in the State of Delaware are required to file an annual report and to pay a franchise tax. Limited liability companies, limited partnerships and general partnerships do not file an annual report but owe an annual tax. The page gives sample forms and instructions, and states that filing fees are due when a request is submitted. This brief states no amounts, because the figures belong to the current page and change.

Two consequences follow from the page. The first is that forming an entity in a state creates a recurring obligation, not a one-time task: a registered agent has to be maintained, and a tax or report is due every year. The second is that a company formed in one state and operating in another may have to register in the second as well. The Delaware page does not describe that, and no other source in the evidence set does either.

This brief has no source on why founders choose one state over another, so it offers no recommendation on the choice. Analysis: the choice of state is one to put to a lawyer who knows both the founders' home state and the expectations of the investors they hope to approach.

## Choice three: how the company is taxed

Legal form and tax treatment are separate choices. The Internal Revenue Service page on limited liability companies says that a domestic limited liability company with at least two members is classified as a partnership for federal income tax purposes, unless it files Form 8832 and elects to be taxed as a corporation. A limited liability company with one member is disregarded as separate from its owner for income tax, and can also elect corporate treatment with the same form.

The same page adds a detail that is easy to miss. For employment taxes and certain excise taxes, a limited liability company with only one member is still considered a separate entity. A founder who thinks of a single-member company as invisible to the tax system is wrong about the payroll side. The page also gives timing rules for the election: it generally cannot take effect more than 75 days before the date it is filed, and it cannot take effect later than 12 months after the date it is filed.

The IRS page on S corporations describes a second election. To become an S corporation, the corporation must submit Form 2553, signed by all the shareholders. The page lists the conditions: no more than 100 shareholders and only one class of stock. It says the permitted shareholders are individuals, certain trusts and estates, and that partnerships, corporations and non-resident alien shareholders are not allowed. It also notes that an S corporation is responsible for tax on certain built-in gains and passive income at the entity level.

Analysis: the conditions are worth reading against a plan to raise money. A rule that allows only one class of stock, and that excludes shareholders that are themselves partnerships or corporations, constrains the kinds of owners and the kinds of shares a company can have. A company that expects investors with different rights, or institutional investors, should ask whether an S corporation election is compatible with that plan before making it. The evidence set does not say whether it is, and this brief does not guess.

The corporate default has its own consequence, which the Small Business Administration guide states: a corporation pays income tax on its own profits. Analysis: the three treatments divide the question of who pays tax on the profits differently, and none of them removes the question. A founder choosing among them is choosing among a company that pays the tax, an owner who pays the tax on the company's profits whether or not they were paid out, and a hybrid with conditions.

## Choice four: dividing the ownership among the founders

A company that has several founders has to say who owns what. The sources in this brief do not describe how founders should divide their shares, and nothing here advises on the division. What they do describe is a deadline attached to one of its consequences.

Treasury Regulation section 1.83-2 sets out how the election under section 83(b) of the Internal Revenue Code is made. The regulation says the election shall be filed not later than 30 days after the date the property was transferred, and it says the election may be filed before the date of transfer. It says the election is made by filing one copy of a written statement with the internal revenue office where the person files a return, and that the person must also give a copy of the statement to the person for whom the services are performed. If that person is not the one who received the property, a copy goes to the recipient as well.

The regulation lists what the statement must contain: it must be signed and say that it is made under section 83(b), and it must give the person's name, address and taxpayer identification number, a description of the property, the date of the transfer and the tax year, the restrictions on the property, the fair market value at the time of the transfer, and any amount paid for the property.

Two points follow from the text. The first is that the 30 days are counted from the transfer, so the clock starts when the shares are issued, not when the founders get around to the paperwork. The second is that the statement lists the restrictions on the property as one of its contents. Analysis: that suggests the election matters most where the shares come with restrictions, such as a schedule under which a founder earns them over time. The regulation does not use the word vesting, and this brief does not explain the tax effect of making or not making the election.

Analysis: this is the clearest example in the brief of a decision that is cheap early and expensive late. The statement is short. The deadline is fixed in the regulation, and a founder should not assume that a missed deadline can be repaired. The question to take to an accountant is whether the election applies to the shares being issued, and the moment to take it is before the shares are transferred, not after.

The regulation is a snapshot. The text on the page can be amended, and a founder should ask the accountant to confirm the current rule and the current form of the statement.

## Choice five: owning what the company makes

A software company's main asset is its code, and the question of who owns it is answered by copyright law. The Copyright Office page says that copyright covers original works of authorship, and it lists computer programs among the examples. It says copyright protects expression, and never ideas, procedures or methods. It also says that copyright exists automatically in an original work of authorship once it is fixed, and that registering a work is not mandatory, though for works of United States origin, registration or refusal is necessary before the owner can enforce copyright in court.

The consequence for a founder is that the code belongs to someone from the moment it is written, and the question is who. The Copyright Act, in section 101, defines a work made for hire in two parts. The first is a work prepared by an employee within the scope of his or her employment. The second is a work specially ordered or commissioned, which counts only if it falls within a list of categories in the statute and only if the parties expressly agree in a written instrument signed by them that the work shall be considered a work made for hire.

The list of categories in the statute names contributions to collective works, parts of audiovisual works, translations, supplementary works, compilations, instructional texts, tests, answer material for tests and atlases. Software is not named in that list.

Analysis: the structure of the definition points to an ordinary difficulty. Code written by an employee in the course of the job fits the first part. Code written by a contractor, or by a co-founder before the company existed, fits it less clearly, and a contractor's work is not made a work made for hire by an agreement unless it also falls within the listed categories. A founder should therefore not assume that paying someone for code gives the company ownership of it. The questions for a lawyer are whether each person who wrote code is an employee for this purpose, and whether a written agreement transferring ownership to the company is needed for those who are not. The sources in this brief do not describe such a transfer, and nothing here says how one is drafted.

Analysis: the cheapest moment to collect such agreements is when the work is done and the people are still working together.

## Choice six: the name

A company needs a name, and a name can be a trademark. The Patent and Trademark Office page says a trademark can be any word, phrase, symbol, design or combination of these things that identifies goods or services. It states that a person is not required to register a trademark, and that a registered trademark provides broader rights and protections than an unregistered one.

The page also says that rights from use are limited to the geographic area where the goods or services are provided, which is the reason registration is described as broader. The page leads to material on searching for similar trademarks before filing, but the content of that material was not part of the evidence for this brief.

Analysis: the practical point is the order of events. A founder who builds a brand for a year and then finds that another business already uses a similar name in the same field has spent the year on a name that cannot be defended. Searching before committing to a name is the cheap step. This brief does not describe patents, the registration process or the grounds on which an application is refused.

## Choice seven: the tax number and the registrations that follow

A company that has employees, operates as a corporation or a partnership, or pays certain taxes needs an employer identification number. The IRS page says that businesses, organizations and some retirement trusts need one to manage their taxes. It describes an online application that is available to a domestic organization whose principal place of business is in the United States, and it requires that the applicant be the responsible party in control of the entity or its authorized representative, with that person's Social Security number or taxpayer identification number to hand. The page says a business whose main location is outside the United States cannot use the online tool and applies another way.

The page also notes a limit of one number per responsible party per day, and a requirement that a third-party designee have signed authorization to apply. It links to a separate page for the full definition of responsible party, and the definition was not part of the evidence for this brief.

Analysis: the application asks for a named person in control of the entity, so the question of who controls the company has to be answered before the number can be requested.

The same pattern holds for the registrations from the second choice. Each is a small filing, and each creates something that has to be maintained: a registered agent, an annual report or tax. The sources in this brief describe the filings and do not describe the consequence of missing one, which should be confirmed with the relevant state.

## The choices side by side

| Choice | What it settles | What stays open | Where the evidence is |
|---|---|---|---|
| Legal form | Who is responsible for the company's obligations | Which form investors will expect | SBA guide |
| State of formation | Which state's law governs and which filings recur | Registration in other states | Delaware page |
| Tax treatment | Who pays tax on the profits | Compatibility with a plan to raise money | IRS pages |
| Founder shares | Who owns what | Whether the 83(b) election applies | Treasury regulation |
| Ownership of the work | Who owns the code | Agreements with contractors and co-founders | Copyright Act, Copyright Office |
| The name | Whether a mark is chosen and protected | Whether anyone else already uses it | USPTO page |
| Tax number | Who controls the entity for tax purposes | Responsible party and filings | IRS EIN page |

Read down the second column and a pattern appears. Analysis: most of these decisions settle a question about people, who is responsible, who owns, who controls, and not a question about paperwork. The paperwork is the record of the decision. A founder who treats the form as the work will complete the form and leave the question open.

## Questions to ask before choosing

These questions test the setup against the company in hand. They do not produce an answer.

**Who will want to see proof, and of what?** A customer may ask for the company's legal name and good standing. An investor may ask who owns the code and how the shares are held. A bank may ask who controls the account. If you cannot say today where each proof would come from, the setup is not finished.

**Which choices have a clock?** The election about shares has one that starts at the transfer. Entity elections have timing rules of their own. Put the dates in a calendar before the events happen.

**Who has written code or made designs without a signed agreement with the company?** Make a list. For each person, ask a lawyer whether an agreement is needed and what it should say.

**What would it cost to change this later?** Changing a legal form, a state or a tax treatment is a project with filings and sometimes tax consequences, and changing a name after customers know it is a cost of another kind. The choice that is hard to change deserves the lawyer's time first.

**Who will read the documents with the founders?** Every choice in this brief produces a document that binds the company, and reading it with a lawyer before signing is cheaper than renegotiating afterward.

## What this brief cannot tell you

This brief cannot tell you which structure, state or tax treatment is right for your company. That depends on facts about your team, your plans and your jurisdiction that no general document can know.

It cannot tell you the current terms. Fees, deadlines, thresholds and forms are the kind of detail that changes, and the evidence for this brief was read on a single day. Check the source before relying on a figure or a date.

It cannot tell you how a rule applies to your facts. The agency pages are summaries for a general audience. The regulation and the statute are the actual text, and applying either to a particular set of facts is a lawyer's or an accountant's work.

It cannot tell you about other jurisdictions. Everything here concerns United States federal rules and the requirements Delaware describes for entities formed there. The rules of other states, other countries and any local license or permit are outside the sources.

It cannot tell you about the topics it does not cover. Patents, employment law, data protection, contracts with customers, insurance and the rules for hiring people in other countries are all outside this brief, and a company will meet most of them.

It cannot tell you how often any choice works out well. The evidence set contains no outcome data, and this brief offers none.

Finally, it is analysis in places, and the analysis can be wrong. The sections above mark where a sentence is a judgment. A founder should treat those sentences as questions to test, not as findings.
