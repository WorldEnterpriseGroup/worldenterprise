*As of 2026-10-10, this is general information, not legal or financial advice. Rules about raising money differ by country and state, and they carry penalties when missed. Ask a lawyer and a tax adviser licensed where your company is formed.*

There is no single way to pay for a company. Founders reach money through customers, savings, grants, loans, individual investors, funds and a few other doors, and each one asks for something different in return. The useful question is rarely "how do I raise money?" It is "which kind of money fits what I can show today, and what does each kind ask me to give?"

This article is a map, not a recommendation. It does not rank the routes, give an amount, predict that any route will work or say which one a particular company should take. It describes what each route is, what it expects of you and which questions are worth bringing to an adviser. The deeper treatment of the instruments sits in the funding desk of this library (see the <MaybeLink path="/insights/topic/funding/">funding and capital desk</MaybeLink>, which lists its articles once they are published).

## Start with what you can show

Money follows evidence, and the evidence available changes with the stage of the company. Write down honestly which of these you hold today:

| What you can show | Examples |
|---|---|
| An idea and a description of the problem | A problem brief, notes from conversations |
| Evidence that people have the problem | Interview records, a waiting list, a commitment to try |
| A working first version | A demo, a pilot, early usage |
| People paying | Invoices, subscriptions, a prepayment |
| A repeatable way to find customers | A channel you can describe and repeat |

The list is a way of seeing where you stand, not a ladder you must climb in order. Some companies skip rungs, and some sit on one for a long time.

## The routes, one by one

### Customer money

Customers can fund a company through sales, pilots, prepayments and paid design work. It is the one route where the person who provides the money is also the person who validates the product. The article on <MaybeLink path="/insights/your-first-ten-customers-a-founder-led-sequence/">founder-led sales to your first ten customers</MaybeLink> looks at how that starts, and the article on customers as capital in this desk covers what to write down before a pilot.

**What it asks of you.** Time spent selling and delivering, and promises you must keep.

**Questions for an adviser.** How should prepayments and deposits be recorded? What do my customer contracts commit me to?

### Your own money, and money from people close to you

Savings, a part-time job that pays the bills, or a loan or gift from family and friends. The U.S. Small Business Administration, in its guidance on the funding request in a business plan, says to specify whether you want debt or equity and the terms you would like applied. That is a useful discipline even for a small amount from someone you know, because a handshake between friends can leave different people with different understandings of what was agreed.

**What it asks of you.** Personal financial risk and, often, a personal relationship you want to protect.

**Questions for an adviser.** Is this a gift, a loan or a purchase of shares? What should be written down, and how is it recorded?

### Grants and research awards

Some public programs fund research and development in small businesses. The SBIR and STTR programs, which America's Seed Fund describes as funding provided through federal agencies to American small businesses, award money to develop technology and chart a path toward commercialization. The programs have their own eligibility rules, calendars and reporting duties, and the technical work must fit a federal agency's topics.

**What it asks of you.** Applications, a defined technical scope, reporting and compliance. Receiving an award does not require giving up shares, but it comes with obligations.

**Questions for an adviser.** Do I meet the eligibility rules? What happens to the intellectual property I develop under an award?

### Loans

A loan is debt: it is repaid, usually with interest, rather than exchanged for shares. The SBA's guidance on funding a business describes investment funds, called Small Business Investment Companies, that are privately owned and managed, licensed and regulated by the SBA, and make both equity and debt investments in qualifying small businesses.

**What it asks of you.** Repayment on a schedule, and often a track record of revenue or assets.

**Questions for an adviser.** What happens if I cannot repay? Does the lender ask for a personal guarantee or security over assets? What does the lender require me to disclose or to maintain?

### Crowdfunding

The SBA describes crowdfunding as raising funds for a business from a large number of people, and says crowdfunders do not generally receive a share of ownership in the business. It adds that every crowdfunding platform is different and tells founders to read the fine print and understand their full financial and legal obligations.

**What it asks of you.** A public promise, and the work of delivering on it.

**Questions for an adviser.** What am I promising backers, and what are my obligations if I cannot deliver?

### Individual investors and venture funds

The SBA states that venture capital is normally offered in exchange for an ownership share and an active role in the company, and that it invests capital in return for equity, rather than debt. It adds that almost all venture capitalists will, at a minimum, want a seat on the board of directors, so you should be prepared to give up some portion of both control and ownership. It also says the process generally follows a standard order of basic steps, beginning with finding an investor.

**What it asks of you.** Shares and some control, a plan that fits an investor's goals, and a good deal of time. See <MaybeLink path="/insights/the-founder-time-budget-of-a-raise/">the founder time budget of a raise</MaybeLink> and <MaybeLink path="/insights/choosing-the-right-first-investors-for-a-saas-company/">choosing the right first investors</MaybeLink>.

**Questions for an adviser.** Which legal route am I using to offer shares? What do I give up in ownership, control and information?

### Standard agreements for early investment

Some early investors fund a company through a short agreement instead of buying shares right away. The best known is published by Y Combinator.

<p data-generic-definition>A SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity) is described by its publisher as a short contract an investor signs to fund a startup now in exchange for the right to shares of stock in the startup later, converting into shares automatically when the startup raises a priced round. This is a description of a published form, not of any term anyone has offered you.</p>

The <MaybeLink path="/fundability/safe-explained/">explainer on SAFEs</MaybeLink> sets out how such a document is read. The terms differ between versions, and you should have counsel read the exact document in front of you.

## Why the law belongs in this decision

Offering shares or similar rights is regulated. In United States law, unless a registration statement is in effect as to a security, it is unlawful for any person, directly or indirectly, to use interstate commerce or the mails to sell that security. The Act's next section lists exempted transactions, including transactions by an issuer not involving any public offering, and whether a given sale qualifies is a matter for counsel. The rule is why "can I just take this person's money for shares?" is a legal question before it is a commercial one. Raising from people in several countries multiplies the questions.

## A question guide in place of a ranking

Put your answers beside each route rather than choosing from a list:

1. **What evidence do I hold?** Match it against the table above.
2. **What am I willing to give?** Time, control, ownership, personal risk, public commitments.
3. **What does the route require that I do not yet have?** A track record, a technical scope, a legal structure, a record-keeping system.
4. **What happens if the plan changes?** Repayment, reporting and obligations to investors continue.
5. **How long can I wait?** Some routes take a long time to arrive and others arrive fast with stricter strings.
6. **Who must I tell?** Co-founders, employers and anyone whose agreement restricts you.
7. **What would I regret not asking?** Write it down and take it to an adviser.

## What this article cannot tell you

It cannot say which route fits your company, how much any route might provide, or whether you can obtain it. The five sources are an SBA guidance page, the public site of the federal SBIR and STTR programs, the publisher's page for one standard form and two sections of the U.S. Securities Act; they describe United States rules and one commonly used form. They do not cover grants or loans in other countries, state securities rules, the conditions of any exemption, tax treatment, or any terms of any instrument. The article gives no amounts, valuations, percentages or timelines, and it does not name any investor or platform. It describes routes without ranking them because the right order depends on your facts.
