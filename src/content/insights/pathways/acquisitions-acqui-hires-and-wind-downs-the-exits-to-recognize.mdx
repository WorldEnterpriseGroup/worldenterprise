*As of 2026-10-10, this is general information, not legal or financial advice. The rules for selling, closing or dissolving a company depend on where it is formed and what its documents say. Ask a lawyer and a tax adviser licensed where the company is formed.*

Most talk about starting a company is about how it begins. A first-time founder often meets the vocabulary of endings when someone else uses it: an offer to "acquire the team," a request to "wind things down," a suggestion to "sell the assets." The words sound alike and mean different things in law, in tax and for the people and customers afterwards.

This article names the ways a company can end, or change hands, and describes what each can involve in the records and filings. It does not say which outcome is likely, which is better or which a given company should seek. It carries no outcome figures, because none of the sources cited measures how often any of these paths occurs.

## Four shapes, side by side

The table uses plain labels. The words in practice overlap, so ask anyone who uses one what they mean, and ask for the answer in writing.

| Shape | What it means in this article | What usually needs a decision |
|---|---|---|
| Sale of the company | A buyer acquires the company's shares or merges with it | Who must approve, how the price is divided among holders, and what the buyer inherits |
| Sale of assets | The company sells some or all of its property, and remains behind as a legal entity | Which assets and liabilities move, and what the remaining company does next |
| Acqui-hire | A buyer or employer takes on some or all of the people, and may or may not buy the product or the company | Who is hired on what terms, what happens to the product and to the shares of everyone who stays behind |
| Wind-down | The company stops operating and closes its affairs, with or without a formal dissolution | Debts, customers, data, tax filings and the company's records |

"Acqui-hire" is used here as a label for the third row, and nothing more. It has no fixed legal meaning, which is why the question to ask is always what is being bought and what is being offered to whom.

## Sale of assets: a vote may be required

When a company sells everything or nearly everything it owns, the law of its home state may require owners to approve. As one example, Delaware's General Corporation Law provides for a corporation to sell, lease or exchange all or substantially all of its property and assets, and refers to a meeting duly called upon at least 20 days' notice. The same section says the board of directors or governing body may abandon such a proposed sale, lease or exchange.

That is one state's law for one kind of company; others differ. The practical point is that a sale which seems like a board matter can also be a matter for owners, and the company's own documents may add conditions. Questions for counsel:

- Is the proposed sale one that requires an owner vote where the company is formed, and what do its charter and any investor agreement add?
- Who holds a vote, and does any class of shares hold a veto?
- How is the price divided among shareholders, debt holders and any other claimants?
- What liabilities does the buyer take, and which stay with the company?
- What happens to the contracts, the employees and the intellectual property? See <MaybeLink path="/insights/ip-contracts-and-access-diligence-for-small-engineering-teams/">intellectual property and access diligence</MaybeLink> for how records of this kind are reviewed.

## Government filings on larger deals

Some acquisitions require a notice to the government before they close. In the United States, the premerger notification statute says no person shall acquire, directly or indirectly, any voting securities or assets of any other person unless both persons meet requirements that the statute sets out. Which conditions trigger those requirements is a question for counsel. A very small company may well fall outside them; whether yours does is a question for counsel. Other countries have their own regimes, and a buyer from abroad may trigger one.

## Tax reporting on a sale of a business's assets

The Internal Revenue Service states that both the seller and the purchaser of a group of assets that makes up a trade or business must use Form 8594 to report such a sale, when certain conditions apply. How the price is allocated among the assets can matter to both sides, and the allocation is an agreement between them. Ask a tax adviser how a particular structure would be taxed for the company and for each shareholder, because the answers differ between a sale of shares and a sale of assets, and between different company types.

## Acqui-hires: questions for people on both sides

Because an acqui-hire concerns people more than property, its questions are closer to employment than to deal law.

For a founder:

- Is the buyer acquiring the company, buying assets, or only offering jobs? If only jobs, what happens to the company, its debts, its investors and its shareholders?
- Who is offered a role, on what terms and from what date? Are all team members offered something, and if not, how is that explained?
- Does the offer require founders to sign new employment or non-compete terms, and what are they?
- Who owns what you built before the start date, and what you build after it?
- What do your investors and other shareholders receive?

For an employee on a team that is being acquired:

- Is my role being offered, and in writing?
- What happens to the shares or options I hold?
- What is the start date, and who pays me until then?

## Winding down: ending an operating company

Not every company ends in a sale. A wind-down is a set of tasks as much as a decision.

On the legal side, Delaware again offers one example. Its statute refers to the adoption of a resolution to that effect by a majority of the whole board, and says a certification of dissolution shall be filed with the Secretary of State. It adds that dissolution of a corporation may be authorized without action of the directors in a case the statute describes. The same chapter says that a dissolved corporation shall nevertheless be continued, for the term of 3 years from such expiration or dissolution, for the purpose of enabling it gradually to settle and close its business and to dispose of and convey its property, and counsel can say what else the chapter provides. In short, dissolving is a legal step and winding up is the work that follows, and the law may give a defined period for it.

On the tax side, the IRS says that you must file a final return for the year you close your business. It also says the IRS cannot close your business account until you have filed all necessary returns and paid all taxes owed, and that how long to keep business records depends on what is recorded in each document. Its page lists further steps, including paying the tax owed, reporting payments to contract workers, and canceling an employer identification number.

A founder can turn those sources into a checklist to review with advisers:

1. **Authority.** Who must approve closing the company, and what do the documents say about notice and votes?
2. **Debts and claims.** Who is owed money, who might make a claim, and in what order are creditors paid?
3. **Customers.** What did you promise, and what notice, refunds or data handovers are owed?
4. **People.** Final pay, benefits, notices and equity paperwork for every employee and contractor.
5. **Data and accounts.** What data you hold, what is deleted or returned, and which accounts are closed in what order.
6. **Intellectual property.** What happens to the code and the brand: kept, sold, licensed or released.
7. **Tax and filings.** Final returns, employer and contractor reports, and the account closures that follow.
8. **Records.** What you keep, for how long, and where.
9. **Communication.** Who is told, in what order, and by whom.

## Recognizing which path you are on

People sometimes drift into one of these paths without choosing it: a conversation with a larger company becomes a team offer, or a pause becomes a closure. Name the path in your own notes, and ask when each document arrives which of the four shapes it belongs to. The <MaybeLink path="/library/seed-data-room-checklist/">seed data room checklist</MaybeLink> lists the records that any later buyer, investor or adviser is likely to ask for, and keeping them tidy helps in every shape.

## What this article cannot tell you

It cannot say whether a company should be sold, closed or merged, how a buyer will value it, or whether an offer is fair. The four sources cited are one state's corporate statute, an IRS page on closing a business, one federal antitrust statute and an IRS page on one tax form. They describe United States rules, with Delaware as the single example of a state; they do not describe the law of other states or countries, limited liability companies, insolvency and bankruptcy law, employment law, securities law or contract terms. The article gives no valuation method, no price, no outcome figure and no timeline beyond the statutory periods quoted. Whether a vote, a filing or a report applies depends on the company's form, its documents and the size of the deal, and a lawyer who has read them is the right person to say.
