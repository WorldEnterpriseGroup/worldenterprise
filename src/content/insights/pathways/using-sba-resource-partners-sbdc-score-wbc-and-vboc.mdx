Before a founder pays for advice, there is a public network built to give it. The U.S. Small Business Administration, or SBA, funds a set of organizations that it calls resource partners: business counseling centers, a large volunteer adviser network, centers for women entrepreneurs and centers for veteran and military entrepreneurs. They are independent of WE and no affiliation is implied, and WE encourages founders to use them early and keep using them. This article explains what each one does, how many people the SBA says they reached, and how a founder can get the most from a first visit.

## The four partners at a glance

The SBA describes its resource partners as a nationwide network offering counseling, training, mentoring, funding recommendations and other small business help, including dedicated centers for veteran-owned and women-owned businesses.

| Partner | What the SBA says it does | Who it serves |
|---|---|---|
| Small Business Development Centers (SBDC) | Counseling and training, with individualized business advising and technical assistance | Existing small businesses and pre-venture entrepreneurs |
| SCORE | The nation's largest network of volunteer, expert business mentors, with workshops and training | Small businesses at any stage |
| Women's Business Centers (WBC) | A national network of entrepreneurship centers offering counseling and training | Women entrepreneurs, and others who ask |
| Veterans Business Outreach Centers (VBOC) | Entrepreneurial development services, workshops, training and counseling | Service members, veterans, National Guard and Reserve members, military spouses and family members |

The SBA describes the services in general terms, and what a given office offers can vary by location, so confirm the specifics with the office before planning around them.

The same release says SCORE had over 10,000 volunteer mentors, that SBDCs helped start over 26,000 new businesses, and that Women's Business Centers assisted more than 87,000 businesses and helped start over 3,300 new businesses. The SBA also reports that VBOCs conducted over 780 Boots to Business classes.

These numbers show that the network is large and in use. They do not show that counseling caused any business to start or survive. People who seek counseling differ from people who do not, and the release does not compare the two groups.

## How a founder can use each partner

**Start with the question, then choose the partner.**

- **A plan, a budget or a financing question.** An SBDC's advisers work on capital, planning, operations, financial management, marketing, exporting and sales. A founder who needs a second opinion on a cash forecast can bring the <MaybeLink path="/tools/metrics-calculator/">metrics calculator</MaybeLink> output and ask what looks wrong.
- **An ongoing conversation with someone who has run a business.** The SBA says SCORE volunteers meet with clients on an ongoing basis and give area-specific advice by email, telephone and video, and that SCORE offers workshops and on-demand courses. Ask for an adviser with experience in software or subscription products.
- **A center built for women entrepreneurs.** The SBA describes Women's Business Centers as a national network of entrepreneurship centers. The data on women-owned employer firms in the <MaybeLink path="/insights/women-and-immigrant-founders-what-the-census-and-research-data-show/">women and immigrant founders</MaybeLink> article shows why such centers exist.
- **A center for military and veteran founders.** The SBA says it has 31 organizations serving as VBOCs, and that they run entrepreneurial workshops, help with business plans and refer clients to lenders and other SBA programs.

The SBA's local assistance tool lists the partners that serve a ZIP code. For a founder outside the United States, these partners serve U.S. businesses, and the <MaybeLink path="/insights/founding-from-outside-the-united-states-what-changes-for-a-remote-founder/">remote founder</MaybeLink> article covers what changes.

## Preparing for a first meeting

A first visit goes further with a few things in hand.

1. A one-page description of the business and the customer it serves.
2. Your cash on hand, monthly costs and any revenue, even if small. The <MaybeLink path="/insights/runway-and-burn-the-arithmetic/">runway arithmetic</MaybeLink> shows how to work them out.
3. The three questions you most need answered.
4. A note of what you have already tried.

Ask the adviser about their own experience, what they would check first, and what they would not advise you to do. Follow up in writing, and write down what you decide. A counseling center is a source of advice, and the decisions stay with you.

Meetings by telephone or video are common, which helps a founder who works from home or whose nearest office is far away. Send any documents a day ahead so the hour is spent on questions, not on reading. If the first adviser is not a fit, ask for another one. A good match matters more than loyalty to the first name you were given, and the network is large enough that a second try is reasonable.

## A year of use, in outline

A founder does not need to see every partner. One pattern that fits a first year looks like this.

| When | Question to bring | Partner to consider |
|---|---|---|
| Month one | Is the business set up sensibly, and what must be registered? | A counseling center near you |
| Month three | What do the first customer conversations say about pricing and sales? | A volunteer adviser with relevant experience |
| Month six | Does the cash forecast hold, and what happens if a customer leaves? | A counseling center, with your cash figures |
| Month twelve | What should the second year look like, and what support remains useful? | Any of the above, plus a program or a peer group |

The table is an illustration of how to space the questions. It is not a requirement of any partner, and a founder with a different need can start anywhere. The point is to return with a changed question each time, so the advice builds on what came before.

## Where this fits the long horizon

Support of this kind is most useful when it is regular, which suits a long project. A founder who checks the cash forecast with an adviser each quarter, joins a workshop in a slow month and returns after the first hire has used the network the way it is built to be used. ## Questions to ask a counseling center

- What experience do your advisers have with software or subscription businesses?
- How often can I meet, and for how long over a year?
- Which workshops or courses are coming up?
- Do you have a conflict of interest, such as a referral arrangement with a lender?
- What do you ask of me between meetings?

## What this article cannot tell you

This article cannot tell you how useful a particular center or adviser will be, what services your local office offers, or whether you qualify for a program aimed at a specific group. The reach figures are the SBA's own counts for 2021, they count clients rather than outcomes, and the release does not say that counseling caused any result. The SBA's pages can change, so confirm each service with the center. It does not describe any program run by WE, it names no partner as endorsed by WE, and it makes no promise about funding or results.
