*General information, not medical advice, and not a way to diagnose yourself or anyone else. Written October 10, 2026 from public agency pages. If you are struggling, a doctor or another qualified health professional is the right person to talk to, and the section near the end says where the agencies point.*

A founder can go a long way on exhaustion. The work is open-ended, the hours are your own, and nobody sends a warning when the load has stopped being sustainable. This article does not tell you how to cope or what to do. It reports what two public bodies say burnout and work stress are, turns one regulator's framework into questions you can ask about your own work, and points to the places the agencies send people for help.

Two limits shape everything that follows. None of the sources was written for founders, and none of them is medical guidance for you in particular. Read them as vocabulary, not as a verdict.

## What burnout is, as one US institute describes it

The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health, part of the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, publishes an online training on burnout aimed at public health workers. Its second module is titled "What burnout is and is not". Three points from it matter here.

First, the status of the word. The module says that, per the ICD-11 classification, burnout is an occupational phenomenon, rather than a medical condition or actual disease. It also calls it "a very serious strain state or condition". That is a statement about classification, not a claim that the experience is minor.

Second, the content. The module describes two core features. The first is exhaustion, "Serious exhaustion that goes beyond typical daily fatigue or low energy levels." The second is a "Strong and persistent negative attitude" toward the demands of the job, including cynicism and a reduced sense of accomplishment.

Third, what it is not. The module separates burnout from fatigue, noting that the "feelings and effects of fatigue tend to last only a few hours or weeks at a time." It separates it from boredom too: "Chronic over-stimulation and overload leads to burnout." And it warns against treating engagement as protection: "Plenty of workers who are heavily engaged also end up being at high risk for or even experiencing burnout."

That last point is worth sitting with if you love what you are building. Caring about the work does not appear, in this source, to be a safeguard.

## Why a founder might read this differently

The module’s examples come from public health workers, who describe "fatigue, fatigue, and more fatigue", and say they "could not care about their work." You will not recognize your own week in every example. What transfers is the structure: a long-lasting depletion, a hardening attitude toward the work, and a sense that effort no longer connects to results.

Some features of founding may make these descriptions easier to meet. A very small company often has no one else who holds the whole picture, and no natural end to the day. Whether that is true of your company is something only you can judge, and this article does not claim it as a finding.

## The causes the institute points to are in the work

The institute’s third module is about why burnout needs attention among public health workers. Two of its sentences describe demands in a way a founder may recognize. One says that "the absence of resources can be the biggest demand that many public health workers face." Another says that "it is the pace, ambiguity, and unpredictability of work demands" that can put a team "into hyperdrive." The module also says that "Even anticipating not having enough resources in the future or the possibility of losing resources can be stressful."

The module’s most useful line for this article is about where responses should aim. It describes a Total Worker Health perspective that emphasizes "using organizational strategies to protect worker health rather than focusing too much on individual strategies". It adds that "we should not expect that they can or should do this long-term", about workers who keep solving problems despite missing resources. For a founder, the organization is the company you run, so "organizational strategies" means the way the work is designed.

## Six questions about the design of your work

The Health and Safety Executive is the UK workplace regulator. It defines stress as "the adverse reaction people have to excessive pressures or other types of demand placed on them." Its Management Standards name six main areas of work design that affect stress levels: demands, control, support, relationships, role and change. The page names the areas but, in the version read for this article, does not describe them, so the questions below are this article’s own wording of what each area could mean for a founder. They are prompts for reflection, not a test.

Notice that every question is about the design of the work, which a founder can inspect and change, not about the founder’s character. The change is rarely dramatic. It might be a shorter list, a decision made once and written down, a date moved, a customer conversation handed to someone else or a stop condition agreed before it is needed. ## Where the agencies point for help

Whether what you are feeling is burnout, stress, something else or a mixture is not a question an article can answer. The National Institute of Mental Health addresses the next step directly. Its fact sheet names a trigger, "If you are struggling to cope, or the symptoms of your stress or anxiety won’t go away", and says that "it may be time to talk to a professional." Both fragments are the institute’s words, and neither is a diagnosis.

A second NIMH page says to "Seek professional help if you are experiencing severe or distressing symptoms that have lasted 2 weeks or more", and lists examples that include difficulty sleeping and loss of interest in things you usually find enjoyable. The same page lists everyday steps such as "Get regular exercise", "Make sleep a priority" and "Stay connected." Those are the institute’s words about mental health in general, not a treatment plan for burnout.

<Callout title="In immediate distress">
  The NIMH fact sheet says "If you are in immediate distress or are thinking about hurting yourself, call or text the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline" and gives an online chat option. 988 is a US service. Outside the United States, a local emergency number or health service is the equivalent, and a doctor can tell you which one applies where you live.
</Callout>

## What this article cannot tell you

This article cannot tell you whether you are experiencing burnout, depression, anxiety or ordinary tiredness. It is not medical advice and not a screening tool, and it does not say how common burnout is among founders, because none of the sources read measures that. The NIOSH modules were written for public health workers, not founders, and the HSE page is UK guidance on employees. The six questions in the table are this article’s own wording. Everything was read on October 10, 2026, and agencies update their pages. A doctor or other qualified professional, not an article, is the right place to take a concern about your health.
