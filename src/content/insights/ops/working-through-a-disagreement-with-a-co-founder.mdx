*General information, not legal or financial advice. Written October 10, 2026. A disagreement between co-founders can touch ownership, contracts and the company’s legal duties, which differ by place and by the documents you signed. Speak to your own counsel before you act on any of it.*

Two people who start a company together usually agree on a great deal at the start and discover the rest as the company grows. A disagreement is not a sign that the partnership has failed. It is a sign that the company has reached a question the founders had not yet answered. What matters is whether it is handled in a way that keeps the facts straight and the relationship intact.

This article describes a process, not a verdict. It does not say who is right in a disagreement, it does not predict how any one ends, and it does not tell you to stay or to leave. It offers a way to separate the question from the feeling around it, and it explains what mediation is, using the words of a US federal agency and a federal court.

## Start by naming the disagreement

Most co-founder disagreements fall into a few kinds, and the kind changes who can help. A founder can disagree about a decision, such as a price, a hire or a feature. Two founders can disagree about roles, meaning who owns which part of the company. They can disagree about pace and commitment, such as hours, outside work or whether to raise money. They can disagree about money, including pay, expenses and the ownership split. And they can disagree about whether to continue together.

Writing the kind down matters because a decision disagreement and an ownership disagreement are different problems. The first is usually settled by a rule the founders agree on. The second may depend on documents already signed, and the third is a conversation about the relationship. Mixing them is how a dispute about a feature turns into one about everything.

## Check what is already written down

Before the argument goes further, read the documents. Founders often remember an agreement more firmly than they wrote it. The questions here are factual. What does the founder agreement say about decisions and roles? What does the vesting schedule say? What has each founder assigned to the company? Do the documents say anything about how a dispute is to be resolved?

Three articles on this site describe the documents in general terms: <MaybeLink path="/insights/founder-equity-splits-and-vesting/">founder equity splits and vesting</MaybeLink>, <MaybeLink path="/insights/founder-ip-assignment-and-confidentiality-agreements/">founder IP assignment and confidentiality agreements</MaybeLink> and <MaybeLink path="/insights/choosing-a-co-founder-or-deciding-not-to/">choosing a co-founder or deciding not to</MaybeLink>. What a particular document means, and whether it can be changed, is a legal question. Who counsel is acting for, the company or one founder, is a question to put to any lawyer before the documents are read.

## Make the disagreement visible and bounded

A disagreement that lives only in conversation grows at the edges. Writing it down shrinks it. A one-page note can hold four things: the question, each founder’s view in that founder’s own words, the facts both accept and the facts they do not. Each founder reads the other’s view and corrects the wording until the other says it is fair. That step is not agreement. It is accuracy, and accuracy is often enough to show that the real question is smaller than the argument.

A decision rule helps with disagreements about decisions. The article on <MaybeLink path="/insights/meetings-async-work-and-decision-records-for-a-small-team/">meetings, async work and decision records</MaybeLink> describes how to write down who decides what and why a choice was made, so that a settled question stays settled. If the argument is about whether to stop or continue a project, <MaybeLink path="/insights/kill-criteria-deciding-in-advance-when-to-stop/">kill criteria</MaybeLink> describes how to agree a stopping rule in advance.

Keep the conversation about the company’s facts and each founder’s expectations. Whatever the disagreement is, it sits on top of a working relationship, and the pressure of a long disagreement is real. The article on <MaybeLink path="/insights/burnout-recognizing-it-and-what-to-change/">burnout and what to change</MaybeLink> describes what two public bodies say about work stress and where they point for help.

## What a mediator is

When founders cannot move on their own, a neutral third party is one option. The US Equal Employment Opportunity Commission describes mediation as "an informal and confidential way for people to resolve disputes" "with the help of a neutral mediator who is trained to help people discuss their differences." It says "The decision to mediate is completely voluntary." And it states that "The mediator does not decide who is right or wrong or issue a decision."

A federal district court program describes the process in similar terms: "Mediation is a flexible, non-binding, confidential process in which a neutral mediator facilitates settlement negotiations." It adds that "The mediator has no power to impose a settlement and does not attempt to coerce a party to accept any proposed terms." On confidentiality, the court page says "Communications made in connection with a mediation ordinarily may not be disclosed to the assigned judge" and to others not involved, unless otherwise agreed.

Both pages are about disputes in specific settings. The EEOC page is about discrimination charges, and the court page is about cases already in litigation. Neither is about co-founders, and neither says how mediation between co-founders would be arranged or whether it would apply. What they supply is a shared vocabulary for the idea: a neutral person, a voluntary process and a mediator who does not decide the outcome. A mediator, in these pages’ terms, helps the people involved talk. The founders still own the result.

## Choosing whom to ask

A founder in a disagreement can turn to several kinds of person, and they do different jobs.

- A friend or an adviser who knows the company may listen well but is not neutral.
- A neutral third party or mediator can help the founders talk, and by the sources’ own terms does not rule on who is right.
- Counsel advises on legal rights and documents, and acts for a particular client.
- A doctor or another qualified professional is the right person for anything affecting health.

Ask any candidate three things before you begin: who they act for, what they are able to do, and what happens to what you tell them. For a mediator, ask how confidentiality works in their process and whether the founders must agree to it in writing.

## What this article cannot tell you

This article cannot tell you who is right, what your documents mean, whether mediation will work or whether a partnership should continue. It rests on two pages, one from a US federal agency and one from a federal court, read on October 10, 2026, and both describe mediation in settings other than co-founder disputes. It covers no jurisdiction’s company law, no remedies, no duties between founders and no rules on leaving a company, and it gives no outcome or time promise. The process described here is a general approach, not drawn from a source. A qualified mediator or counsel is the right person to ask about a real disagreement.
