Every first product has a positioning statement, whether or not anyone wrote it down. If nobody did, it exists in pieces: one version in your head, another on the website and a third in whatever you say when a stranger asks what you do. Customers hear the differences between those versions as confusion. This article gives you a one-page method for writing the statement down once, in plain words, so that a landing page, an email and a sales call can all be checked against it.

You do not need to write code or hold a marketing title to do this. The method needs a pencil, one page and a few conversations with people who might buy.

## What a positioning statement is, and is not

In this article, a positioning statement is an internal document of five short parts that says who the product is for, what situation they are in, what they do today, what your product does differently and what evidence supports that. It is the working definition used here, a method rather than an industry standard, and you may adapt it.

It is not a tagline and it is not the text you publish. Nobody outside the company needs to read it. Its job is to make later writing decisions faster, because you can ask of any sentence: does this agree with the page?

## The five parts

Fill in one line for each part, in this order.

| Part | The question it answers | Check |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Customer | Who, specifically, has the problem? | You could find ten of them this week |
| 2. Situation | What is happening when they feel it? | A person could say it in their own words |
| 3. Alternative | What do they do today instead? | It is a real habit, not "nothing" |
| 4. Difference | What does your product change about that habit? | One change, stated as something they can do |
| 5. Evidence | What have you actually seen or measured? | Each item points to a record |

Part 3 is the one most often skipped. Nearly every buyer has a current way of coping, even if it is a spreadsheet, an email thread or a colleague who does the task by hand. Your product competes with that habit before it competes with any other product. Name the habit, and you learn what you are really asking someone to give up.

Part 5 is the second most skipped. At the earliest stage the evidence can be small: three interview notes, one pilot, a list of the questions people asked first. Write what exists. If the line is empty, that is useful information, and the [customer interview guide](/library/customer-interview-guide/) and the [validate-the-problem playbook](/playbooks/validate-the-problem/) are the places to fill it.

## An invented example

The example below is made up for this article. It describes no real product, company or customer.

Suppose a founder is building a tool that tracks which client files a small design studio is still waiting on.

| Part | Draft |
|---|---|
| Customer | Owners of design studios with two to ten people who run client projects by email |
| Situation | A project stalls because nobody can say which logos, copy or approvals are still missing from the client |
| Alternative | A shared spreadsheet that one person updates when they remember, plus reminder emails written by hand |
| Difference | The studio sends one link; the client uploads each missing item against a checklist and the owner sees what is still open |
| Evidence | Four conversations in which the owner described a stalled project; no product usage yet |

Notice what the draft does not contain. It has no claim about growth, saving time or being the best option, and the evidence line admits that nothing has been used yet. A statement like this is honest about what is known, and that makes it easy to improve as facts arrive.

## From five parts to one paragraph

Once the table is filled, read it aloud as a paragraph: "For [customer], who [situation], the usual approach is [alternative]. [Product] [difference]. So far, [evidence]." If any sentence needs a long pause or a technical word, that part is not finished.

Two writing rules from public guidance help here. The plain-language guidance published by the U.S. General Services Administration through Digital.gov states that "The first rule of plain language is: write for your audience." The W3C Web Accessibility Initiative's writing tips say to "Use simple language and formatting, as appropriate for the context." Both point the same way. The paragraph should use the words your buyer uses, which you will have heard in interviews, not the words your team uses among themselves.

## Four ways a draft goes wrong

- **The customer is everyone.** "Small businesses" or "teams" is a category of millions. If you cannot name where ten of them gather, whether a community, a directory or a job title, the line is too wide to write to.
- **The situation is a feature in disguise.** "They need a dashboard" describes your product, not their week. Rewrite it as an event: what happened, who noticed, what they did next.
- **The difference is a list.** Three changes read as none. Pick the one that a buyer would notice first and move the others into a notes section.
- **The evidence is a feeling.** "People seem excited" is not a record. Replace it with what someone said, did or agreed to, and the date.

None of these needs a market study to fix. Each is fixed by another conversation and a shorter sentence.

## Test the page on three buyers

A statement is a hypothesis about how buyers see their own situation. Test it before you build text on top of it.

1. Read the customer and situation lines, only those, to someone who fits. Ask, "Is that your week?" Listen for the correction.
2. Read the alternative line. Ask them to show or describe how they do it now. The detail they add is better than anything you invented.
3. Read the difference line last, and ask what they would still need to know before trying it.

Do this with three people and change the page after each one. If all three corrected the same part, that part was wrong. If each corrected a different part, you may be describing several customers at once; choose one for now and keep the others in a note. The earlier piece on [customer discovery interviews](/insights/customer-discovery-interviews-without-false-positives/) explains how to ask in a way that avoids polite agreement.

## Where the statement goes next

Keep the page where everyone working on the product can find it, and date it. When you write a landing page, the [landing page article](/insights/writing-a-landing-page-that-says-what-the-product-does/) shows how each part maps to a section a visitor can read in a few seconds. When you choose where to look for the first customers, the [channels article](/insights/choosing-your-first-channels/) starts from part 1, because a channel is only as good as the share of its audience that matches.

Revisit the statement whenever evidence changes. If a customer you did not expect keeps buying, or the alternative turns out to be a different habit than you thought, edit the page and note the date. The history of edits is a record of what you learned. A term such as ICP, short for ideal customer profile, is defined in the [glossary](/glossary/) if you want the shorthand investors and advisors use for part 1.

## What this article cannot tell you

This article does not tell you whether your positioning is right. It gives you a way to write it down and a way to test it with a few people, and a handful of conversations cannot show how a whole market sees your product. The five-part method is an editorial working definition, not a standard from any body or a model taken from a named framework, and other methods may suit your product better. The two cited sources concern plain writing and accessibility; they do not address positioning itself, and they were read on 2026-10-10. Nothing here depends on your country, your industry or the size of your company, but a regulated product may need its claims reviewed by a qualified professional before any of them is published.
