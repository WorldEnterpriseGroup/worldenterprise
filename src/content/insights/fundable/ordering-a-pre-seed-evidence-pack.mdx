Here, "ordering" an evidence pack means putting it in order, not buying one. A pack is a set of records that back the statements a founder makes about a company, arranged so that a reader who was not there can find each record without help. This article describes how the site's own materials arrange that set, why each arrangement exists, and which questions are worth taking to counsel and an accountant. It describes preparation. It does not say what any investor will ask for, and nothing in a pack predicts what anyone will do with it.

## What a pack is made of

The unit is a pair: a statement and the record that shows it. The [investor readiness checklist](/fundability/investor-readiness-checklist/) is built this way. It lists twelve statements a founder can answer yes or no, and for each one it names the artifact another person could open. A statement without an artifact stays a claim, a distinction the article on [evidence over narrative](/insights/evidence-over-narrative-what-diligence-actually-tests/) develops.

The twelve statements fall into four groups, and the order of the groups is the first ordering the materials offer:

| Group | What it evidences | Artifacts the checklist names |
|---|---|---|
| The problem and the customer | That a real problem exists and that you learned about it from the people who have it | A one-page problem brief; interview notes and a short summary |
| The product and the first customers | That people use the product, that you can count what happens, and that growth is not a one-off | A released version and a note of use; a customer conversation log; a metrics definitions sheet; a channel log |
| The company and its records | That the company exists on paper, owns what it sells and keeps its records in order | Signed assignments; formation documents and a current cap table; a data room folder with an index; a data inventory and an access list |
| Communication and counsel | That you report progress consistently and understand what you may be asked to sign | Two or more dated updates in the same format; a written list of questions for your counsel |

The groups run from the problem outward to the company. The [one-page problem brief](/library/one-page-problem-brief/) and the [customer interview guide](/library/customer-interview-guide/) are templates for the first group's artifacts.

## A second ordering: folders

A statement list answers what you claim. A folder structure answers where each record lives. The guide to [the seed data room](/fundability/data-room/) describes nine folders: company, product, customers, metrics and finance, people and contracts, intellectual property, security and privacy, legal, and updates. The [seed data room checklist](/library/seed-data-room-checklist/) lists seven sections of the same material, with a status of present, missing or not applicable and an owner for each item.

The two orderings answer different questions, and the same record can sit in both. A signed assignment is the artifact for a company-group statement and also a document in a people-and-contracts folder. A pack can keep one master copy in the folder structure and refer to it from the statement list, which avoids holding two versions of a record that can drift apart.

## The index comes first

The data room guide gives a test for the whole arrangement: a stranger should find any document in under a minute using the index alone. An index is the document that makes the pack a pack, because it states what is in each folder, the date of each file and what is missing. The guide also says a short note in the index naming a missing record and when it is expected is easier to trust than a quiet gap, and that a plan should be labelled as a plan.

These are the guide's own rules and not legal requirements. They describe a way of keeping an index honest. Whether a particular record should be included at all, or in what form, is a question for counsel.

## A third ordering: who looks first

Some records need a professional's eyes before anyone else sees them. The guide says to ask counsel to review the company, people and contracts, intellectual property and legal folders before they go anywhere, and to remove from any document with personal data what counsel says must be removed. It also says to share the minimum, in stages, and to keep a log of who was given access and when.

That gives a third ordering, by review and exposure: which folders counsel reads first, which are shared early, and which wait. The data room checklist ends with a short list of checks along the same lines, including that someone other than the author has checked the cap table against the signed documents.

## Records that are not about investors at all

Some records exist for reasons that have nothing to do with a raise. The IRS's page on recordkeeping says good records help you monitor the progress of your business, prepare financial statements, keep track of deductible expenses and support items reported on your tax returns. It says you may choose any recordkeeping system suited to your business that clearly shows your income and expenses and that, except in a few cases, the law does not require any special kind of records.

It also says you must keep your records as long as needed to prove the income or deductions on a tax return, and to keep all records of employment taxes for at least four years. Those statements describe tax records. How long a given company keeps a given document, and how the tax folder in a pack relates to the rest of its books, is a question for an accountant.

## Showing the security and privacy answers

The [security baseline checklist](/library/security-baseline-checklist/) is a self-assessment in six sections: accounts and access, code and build, data, product security, vendors, and logging and response. Each question is answered yes, partly or no, with an owner and a date. The checklist says it does not certify anything and maps loosely to public references. One of them, the Secure Software Development Framework, is described by NIST as a set of fundamental, sound and secure software development practices based on established practice documents from organizations such as BSA, OWASP and SAFECode.

In a pack, those answers show what the team says it does and knows, and no more. A self-assessment is not a certification, and the checklist says so itself. The related article on [answering a security and privacy questionnaire](/insights/answering-a-security-and-privacy-questionnaire/) describes the formats in which these questions often arrive.

## Pairing the pack with the documents counsel owns

The company and legal folders depend on documents the articles in the legal desk describe. For example, [founder IP assignment and confidentiality agreements](/insights/founder-ip-assignment-and-confidentiality-agreements/) explains the signed instrument behind the assignments statement, and [founder equity splits and vesting](/insights/founder-equity-splits-and-vesting/) describes the records behind a cap table. The article on [IP, contracts and access diligence](/insights/ip-contracts-and-access-diligence-for-small-engineering-teams/) explains how those records are read together.

## Questions to bring to counsel and an accountant

The [questions to ask your counsel before you sign](/insights/questions-to-ask-your-counsel-before-you-sign/) is a general list. For a pack:

1. Which documents in the company, people, intellectual property and legal folders should counsel read before anything is shared, and what must be removed?
2. Does the cap table match the signed documents, and who checked it?
3. Which agreements limit what we may disclose, such as customer contracts with confidentiality terms?
4. How should personal data in a document be handled before it enters a shared folder?
5. Which financial and tax records belong in the pack, and how long do we keep each?
6. Who owns each folder, and who updates it when something changes?
7. How do we label a plan, a draft and a gap so no reader mistakes one for a signed record?
