Founders often ask how much money to raise. A better question, asked first, is how much of the company's most limited resource a raise will use. That resource is not cash. It is founder attention. A small company has a small number of people who can make the product, serve customers and decide what to do next, and the same people are usually the ones who must talk to investors. This article treats the raise as a project with a time budget, so the decision to start one is made with open eyes.

## Why time, not money, is the cost to plan for

Paul Graham's essay on how to raise money states the issue sharply: when you start fundraising, everything else tends to grind to a halt, and the problem is less the hours consumed than the fact that fundraising becomes the top idea in your mind. His practical advice is to be either fundraising or not fundraising, and to avoid splitting attention. He also suggests that one founder handle the fundraising so that the others can stay focused on the product and the customers.

You may disagree with parts of that advice, and the essay is from 2013 and written from one vantage point. But the underlying observation is useful: the cost of a raise includes the effect on everything you would otherwise be doing. A budget has to account for it.

## What a raise is made of

No two processes are alike, and we cannot say how long yours will take. What we can do is list the kinds of work involved, so that you can estimate each one for your own situation.

| Work | What it involves | Who can do it |
|---|---|---|
| Preparation | Records, metric definitions, a clear account of the business and what you will use funds for | Founders, with help for documents |
| Legal groundwork | Choosing a route, preparing documents, filings | Your counsel, with founder decisions |
| Conversations | Meetings, follow-ups, questions and answers | Mostly founders |
| Diligence responses | Providing documents and explanations on request | Founders and whoever holds the records |
| Negotiation | Discussing terms | Founders, with counsel |
| Closing | Signing, filings and record updates | Counsel and founders |
| Ongoing communication | Updates to people who have already committed | Founders |

Notice how much sits in the "mostly founders" column. Counsel can prepare paper, but only you can talk about your product with conviction. That is why the time budget matters.

## Estimating without invented numbers

We do not give a standard number of hours or weeks, because we know of no public primary source that would fit your situation, and an invented figure would be misleading. Build your own estimate in four steps.

1. **List the work packages** from the table above and add any that are particular to your company.
2. **Estimate each one as a range,** a low and a high, in founder hours. Write the reasoning beside each range.
3. **Add a margin for the unexpected,** because unexpected requests are the rule.
4. **Compare the total to your real weekly capacity** after customers, product and operations have taken what they need.

The result is not a forecast. It is a way to see whether the plan is possible without damaging the company. If the total looks impossible, that is useful information: it may mean raising now is the wrong choice, or that the scope has to change.

## Protect the core

Before you start, name what must not slip while you raise. For most early software companies, three things qualify:

- **Customer commitments.** Support, delivery and the weekly rhythm with your first customers.
- **The product's core loop.** Whatever drives the value customers pay for.
- **The team's direction.** A clear weekly plan, owned by someone other than the person raising.

Write these down and assign each an owner. If you are the only founder, be especially careful: be honest about which of these you will be unable to do during the raise, and what you will cut.

## Delegate and prepare what you can

Some work does not need a founder.

- **Records.** Keep them up to date as part of normal operations, so that preparation is a matter of collecting rather than creating.
- **Legal paper.** Let counsel draft and file. The SEC describes the exemption routes and filings small companies use for securities offerings, which is a reminder that the paperwork has real requirements. Counsel should own that work.
- **Scheduling and logistics.** Use a simple tool and a template message.
- **Tracking.** A shared sheet of conversations, with dates, questions asked and promises made, saves you from reconstructing them from memory.

What you cannot delegate is the substance of the conversation and the decisions. Plan your own hours around them.

## Set a stopping rule before you start

A raise without an end point can drift. Before the first conversation, write down:

- **The date by which you will decide whether to continue.** Choose a date and a reason, not a feeling.
- **What you will do if it takes longer than planned.** Options might include reducing the scope, pausing, or continuing with a smaller amount of outside money or none.
- **The minimum your company needs to operate.** This tells you whether any particular offer is acceptable.
- **What you will not accept.** Terms you cannot live with, written down in advance with your counsel, are easier to hold to than ones you work out under pressure.

None of these guarantee a result. They make the decision to stop, or to carry on, a deliberate one.

## Keep the company moving

Some founders find that keeping a simple weekly measure visible helps hold attention on the business. Choose one number that reflects real progress, such as paying customers or committed pilots, define it in writing and report it weekly to your team. Our article on the first ten customers covers how to define one. If the number stalls during the raise, that is a signal to look at your allocation of time.

## Communicate with those already involved

If you have advisers, early supporters or customers who know you are raising, tell them what to expect and when. A short update on a regular schedule takes less time than answering scattered questions, and it is also a record. Our weekly update template in the library is a starting format. Be accurate in these updates, and be careful about what you say regarding an offering; ask counsel what is appropriate to share.

## Alternatives are part of the budget

Raising outside money is not the only option, and it is not required. Some companies grow from customer revenue, and some use other sources, each with different costs and constraints. Time spent on a raise is time not spent on those. When you make your budget, include the alternative of not raising and compare, using the same approach: what would it cost in attention, and what would it protect?

## What this article does not know

We cannot tell you how long a raise takes, how many conversations it needs or whether it will succeed. We have no public primary source for those numbers and have not included any. The advice in the cited essay reflects its author's experience and is not a rule. World Enterprise makes no promise of funding, and nothing here suggests that preparing your time makes any outcome more likely. It only helps you decide, with open eyes, whether to spend the time.
