The NIST AI Risk Management Framework, usually shortened to the AI RMF, is a document from the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology about managing the risks of artificial intelligence systems. It is written for organizations of every size, and a small team that ships a feature built on a language model will find its vocabulary useful and its length daunting. This article describes what the framework contains, offers one way to read it at the scale of a few people, and says what it leaves out.

It describes a published document, as the NIST pages read on October 10, 2026. It does not state that any company follows it, conforms to it or has been assessed against it.

## What the framework is

NIST describes the AI RMF as a framework developed to better manage risks to individuals, organizations and society associated with AI, and says it is intended for voluntary use. It was released on January 26, 2023. The document itself calls the framework "voluntary, rights-preserving, non-sector-specific, and use-case agnostic", with flexibility for organizations of all sizes and in all sectors.

The framework has two parts. The first frames AI risk and describes the characteristics of a trustworthy AI system: valid and reliable, safe, secure and resilient, accountable and transparent, explainable and interpretable, privacy-enhanced, and fair with harmful bias managed. The second part is the core, which describes four functions, and the profiles that apply it to a setting.

One point is easy to miss and shapes everything below. NIST's AI RMF page says that the AI RMF 1.0 is being revised as part of the White House AI Action Plan, and the Playbook page says it will be updated after the framework is revised. Treat the current text as a snapshot.

## The four functions

The core organizes AI risk management into four functions: Govern, Map, Measure and Manage. The document says that Govern applies to all stages of an organization's AI risk management, while the other three can be applied in AI system-specific contexts and at specific stages of the AI lifecycle. It also warns that the actions under the functions "do not constitute a checklist, nor are they necessarily an ordered set of steps."

Some subcategories in the document read as plainly useful at any size. Govern 2.1 asks that roles, responsibilities and lines of communication for mapping, measuring and managing AI risks are documented and clear to individuals and teams throughout the organization. Map 1.1 asks that intended purposes, potentially beneficial uses, specific laws, norms and expectations, and prospective deployment settings are understood and documented. Measure 2.1 asks that test sets, metrics and details about the tools used during testing, evaluation, verification and validation are documented. Manage 2.4 asks that mechanisms are in place, with responsibilities assigned, to supersede, disengage or deactivate AI systems that demonstrate performance or outcomes inconsistent with intended use. Manage 4.3 asks that incidents and errors are communicated to relevant AI actors, and that processes for tracking, responding to and recovering from them are followed and documented.

On measurement the framework is direct. The Measure function says AI systems should be tested before their deployment and regularly while in operation. ## The Playbook

NIST publishes a companion Playbook that provides suggested actions for achieving the outcomes in the core. The Playbook page states that it is neither a checklist nor a set of steps to be followed in its entirety, and that its suggestions are voluntary. It is the place to look for concrete practices once a subcategory has caught your attention, and the page notes that it will be updated after the framework is revised.

## The Generative AI Profile

On July 26, 2024 NIST released NIST AI 600-1, the Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile. The document explains that a profile is an implementation of the AI RMF functions, categories and subcategories for a specific setting, application or technology, and here the technology is generative AI. It lists twelve risks it describes as unique to or exacerbated by generative AI:

- CBRN information or capabilities
- confabulation
- dangerous, violent or hateful content
- data privacy
- environmental impacts
- harmful bias or homogenization
- human-AI configuration
- information integrity
- information security
- intellectual property
- obscene, degrading and abusive content
- value chain and component integration

Three of these describe situations a small team building on a third-party model is likely to meet. The profile defines confabulation as "the production of confidently stated but erroneous or false content". It describes human-AI configuration as arrangements of a human and an AI system that can result in, among other things, automation bias or over-reliance. And it describes value chain and component integration as including non-transparent or untraceable integration of upstream third-party components and improper supplier vetting. The first is a reason to test, the second a reason to keep a person in the loop (see <MaybeLink path="/insights/human-review-in-an-ai-feature-where-to-keep-a-person-in-the-loop/">human review in an AI feature</MaybeLink>), and the third a reason to ask hard questions of a provider (see <MaybeLink path="/insights/choosing-a-model-provider-the-questions-that-matter/">choosing a model provider</MaybeLink>). For the information security entry, see <MaybeLink path="/insights/prompt-injection-and-the-owasp-top-10-for-llm-applications/">prompt injection and the OWASP Top 10</MaybeLink>.

## A one-page worksheet

The framework does not prescribe a template, so any worksheet is your own. One page per AI feature, with these headings, covers a lot of what the subcategories above ask for in spirit.

1. **Purpose and users.** What is the feature for, who uses it and what do they expect?
2. **Owner.** Who decides that it ships, and who can switch it off?
3. **Components.** Which models, data sources and third-party services does it depend on?
4. **Tests.** Which test sets and metrics did you run, with what results, on what date?
5. **Human oversight.** Where does a person review or override the output?
6. **Incidents.** How are errors recorded and communicated, and how is the feature rolled back?
7. **Review date.** When will you read this page again?

## What this article cannot tell you

The AI RMF and its Generative AI Profile are voluntary U.S. federal documents. They are not law, and they do not tell you what any statute or regulation requires of your product; that is a question for counsel, and the article on <MaybeLink path="/insights/ai-regulation-for-a-small-saas-company-what-to-ask-counsel/">AI regulation for a small SaaS company</MaybeLink> lists questions to bring. The framework is under revision, so details here may change. This article summarizes the text as read on October 10, 2026 and does not reproduce it.

Nothing in this article states or implies that any organization follows, conforms to or is certified under the framework or the profile. The worksheet is a suggestion by this article, not NIST guidance. This is general information, current as of October 10, 2026, and is not legal or financial advice.
