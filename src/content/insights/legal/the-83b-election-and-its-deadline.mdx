*General information as of October 10, 2026. It is not legal or tax advice, and it describes United States federal sources only. A tax professional can say whether the election applies to a particular grant and what its deadline is on a particular set of facts.*

The deadline attached to the 83(b) election is set by the statute at 30 days, and the sources read for this article describe no later extension. This article describes what the election is, what the statute, the regulation and the IRS's own form say about when and how it is made, and which questions are worth taking to a tax professional before shares are issued. It does not say whether anyone should make the election, because that depends on facts these sources cannot see.

## Where the election comes from

The election lives in section 83 of the Internal Revenue Code, which covers property transferred in connection with the performance of services. The default rule in subsection (a) is that the value of such property is included in the gross income of the person who performed the services in the first taxable year in which that person's rights in the property are transferable or are not subject to a substantial risk of forfeiture, whichever applies. The companion article on [founder equity splits and vesting](/insights/founder-equity-splits-and-vesting/) describes those two terms.

Subsection (b) offers a different timing. It says any person who performs services in connection with which property is transferred may elect to include in gross income for the taxable year in which the property is transferred the excess, if any, of the fair market value of the property at the time of transfer over the amount paid for it. The statute says the value is determined without regard to any restriction other than one which by its terms will never lapse.

In the IRS's words in Publication 525, a person who makes the choice finds that the substantial vesting rules do not apply and that, generally, any later appreciation in value is not included in compensation when the property becomes substantially vested. The regulation adds that paying full value for the property, realizing no bargain element, does not preclude use of the election.

## What the sources say about the deadline

The statute at section 83(b)(2), the regulation at 26 CFR 1.83-2(b), the Form 15620 instructions and Publication 525 all give the same period: the election is filed not later than 30 days after the date the property was transferred. The regulation adds that it may be filed before the date of transfer.

The Form 15620 instructions add one rule about weekends. They say that, under section 7503 of the Code, if the thirtieth day following the transfer falls on a Saturday, Sunday or legal holiday, the election is considered timely filed if it is postmarked by the next succeeding day that is not a Saturday, Sunday or legal holiday.

The clock runs from the date the property was transferred. What counts as the date of transfer for a given grant is a question for a tax professional; the form's instructions point to the regulation on transfers.

The sources read for this article describe no extension of the 30 days. Whether any relief exists for a late filing, and on what facts, is not something they answer, so it is a question for a tax professional and not a point to assume either way.

## How the election is made

The regulation says the election is made by filing one copy of a written statement with the internal revenue office with which the person who performed the services files their return. The IRS publishes Form 15620, titled Section 83(b) Election; the revision read for this article is dated April 2025. Publication 525 and the form's own instructions both say the choice can be made by filing a written statement or Form 15620. The instructions say that in the alternative an election may be made by a written statement that satisfies the requirements of the regulation.

The regulation lists what the statement must contain:

1. The name, address and taxpayer identification number of the taxpayer.
2. A description of each property for which the election is made.
3. The date or dates on which the property is transferred and the taxable year for which the election is made.
4. The nature of the restriction or restrictions to which the property is subject.
5. The fair market value at the time of transfer, determined without regard to any lapse restriction, of each property.
6. The amount, if any, paid for the property.
7. A statement to the effect that copies have been furnished to other persons as the regulation provides.

It also requires that the statement be signed by the person making the election and that it say it is being made under section 83(b). Form 15620 collects the same information in numbered boxes.

On where to send it, the form's instructions say to submit the completed and signed form by mail to the IRS office with which the person who performs the services files a federal income tax return.

Copies are part of the process. The regulation and the instructions say the person who performed the services also submits a copy to the person for whom the services are performed, and, if the person who performs the services and the transferee are not the same person, a copy to the transferee. Where the company is the party for whom the services are performed, a copy goes to it. Which copies apply to a particular grant is a question for counsel.

## What the election does not do

Three limits appear in the sources, and each is worth reading in the original.

**It is hard to undo.** The statute says the election may not be revoked except with the consent of the Secretary. The regulation says consent will be granted only where the transferee is under a mistake of fact as to the underlying transaction, and that it must be requested within 60 days of the date the mistake first became known. It adds that a mistake as to value, or a decline in value, of the property, or a failure to perform an act contemplated at the time of transfer, does not constitute a mistake of fact. The Form 15620 instructions refer readers to that regulation and to Revenue Procedure 2006-31.

**It carries a forfeiture consequence.** The statute says that if the property is subsequently forfeited, no deduction is allowed in respect of the forfeiture. The regulation and Publication 525 describe the loss on forfeiture as the amount paid for the property minus the amount realized on the forfeiture, and the regulation says the loss is a capital loss if the property is a capital asset. How those statements apply together to a particular company's shares is a question for a tax professional.

**It does not reach options.** Publication 525 says you can't make this choice for a statutory or nonstatutory stock option. 

## Why the topic reaches founders so early

When a company issues shares to founders under conditions tied to continued service, as the article on [founder equity splits and vesting](/insights/founder-equity-splits-and-vesting/) describes, the date of transfer is the date from which the 30 days are counted. How ownership is written is the subject of the article on [choosing an entity](/insights/choosing-an-entity-llc-or-c-corporation/), which comes first. Whether the election is relevant to a given arrangement, including one in an LLC or one where founders pay full value, is a tax question the sources do not settle.

A reviewer reading a company's file later may look for evidence that a filing was made and when. The [seed data room checklist](/library/seed-data-room-checklist/) shows where such records are kept, and the article on [founder IP assignment and confidentiality agreements](/insights/founder-ip-assignment-and-confidentiality-agreements/) covers another document a founder may be asked to sign around the time shares are issued.

## Questions to bring to a tax professional

The [questions to ask your counsel before you sign](/insights/questions-to-ask-your-counsel-before-you-sign/) offers a general list. For this topic, these are the specific ones:

1. Is any property being transferred to me in connection with services, and is it subject to a substantial risk of forfeiture?
2. On what date is the property transferred, and when does the 30-day period end on that date?
3. What is the fair market value at the time of transfer, how is it determined, and what amount am I paying?
4. Is the election relevant to my situation, and what are the consequences either way, including if the shares are later forfeited?
5. Do I file Form 15620 or a written statement, and where, and by what method is the filing sent?
6. Which copies go to the company or to any other person, and who keeps the record of what was sent?
7. If the facts change after filing, what does the revocation rule allow?

## What this article cannot tell you

It does not say whether you should make the election. Whether it helps or hurts depends on the value of the shares at transfer, on what is paid, on the conditions attached and on your own tax position, none of which the sources read here can supply. It gives no tax rates and no estimate of any tax.

It describes federal income tax rules only. State tax treatment, treatment outside the United States, and equity plans are outside its scope. It does not describe how to determine fair market value, which is a subject for professionals, and it does not say whether a late filing can be cured.

The sources were read on October 10, 2026. Forms, instructions and regulations are revised from time to time, and the Form 15620 read here is the April 2025 revision, so a filing should rest on the current form and on advice from a tax professional who has the actual documents.
