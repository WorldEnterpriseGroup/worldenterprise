Founders often sort investors into angels, micro-funds and funds, and ask how each kind decides. No source cited here describes how any of them decides, so this article does not guess. What public sources do describe is the legal frame around every one of these investors: who counts as an accredited investor, which exemptions let a company sell them shares, and what the regulator tells investors to ask before they put money in. Those rules shape what you must do whoever writes the check. For the practical side of choosing, read [choosing the right first investors for a SaaS company](/insights/choosing-the-right-first-investors-for-a-saas-company/).

## The legal question behind the labels

A private company that sells shares must either register the offering or rely on an exemption. The SEC says every offer and sale of securities must either be registered under the Securities Act of 1933 or rely on an available exemption from registration. Offerings that rely on Securities Act Section 4(a)(2) or its safe harbor under Regulation D are often called private placements, and the SEC's investor bulletin notes that companies, from small start-ups to large, well-established ones, use them to raise funds.

Regulation D leans on a defined term, the accredited investor. The rule defines it as a person who comes within a listed category, or whom the issuer reasonably believes comes within one, at the time of the sale. So the question to ask of any investor is not whether they call themselves an angel or a fund. It is which category, if any, they fall into.

## How a person qualifies

The rule, in 17 CFR 230.501(a), lists the paths for an individual. A natural person qualifies if they have:

- an individual net worth, or joint net worth with a spouse or spousal equivalent, above $1,000,000, with the primary residence left out of the calculation under conditions the rule sets;
- individual income above $200,000 in each of the two most recent years, or joint income above $300,000 with a spouse or spousal equivalent, and a reasonable expectation of reaching the same level this year;
- in good standing, a professional certification, designation or credential that the SEC has designated, which the SEC's bulletin says includes a Series 7, 65 or 82 license.

The rule also lists any director, executive officer or general partner of the company selling the securities. That category matters to you: a founder who is a director or executive officer of their own company is covered by the rule's text.

## How an entity qualifies

An investment vehicle is an entity, and the rule gives entities their own paths. The table is a simplified reading of some of them. The rule carries conditions and exceptions that this table leaves out.

| Path | What the rule says |
|---|---|
| Large entity | An organization, corporation, trust, partnership or limited liability company, not formed for the specific purpose of acquiring the securities offered, with total assets above $5,000,000 |
| Owned by accredited investors | Any entity in which all of the equity owners are accredited investors |
| Entity with investments | An entity of a type not listed elsewhere, not formed for the specific purpose of acquiring the securities, owning investments above $5,000,000 |
| Regulated institutions | Banks, registered brokers and dealers, registered investment advisers, insurance companies, and a Small Business Investment Company licensed by the U.S. Small Business Administration, among others |

A vehicle formed for the specific purpose of acquiring the securities is treated differently. For counting purchasers under Regulation D, an entity organized for the specific purpose of acquiring the securities, and not accredited as an entity owned wholly by accredited investors, is looked through: each beneficial owner counts as a separate purchaser. Ask any investor that writes through a special-purpose vehicle how the vehicle qualifies.

## What the exemption path asks of you

The exemption a company relies on sets who may invest and what the company must do.

- **Rule 506(b).** The company may sell to an unlimited number of accredited investors and to no more than 35 non-accredited investors in any 90-calendar-day period. It may not generally solicit.
- **Rule 506(c).** The company may generally solicit or advertise, but only accredited investors may buy, and the company has to take reasonable steps to verify each one's status.
- **Disclosure.** If the company offers securities to non-accredited investors, it must disclose certain information about itself, including its financial statements. If it sells only to accredited investors, it has discretion over what to disclose, and any information given to accredited investors must also be given to non-accredited ones.
- **Form D.** Every company relying on a Regulation D exemption must file a Form D no later than 15 days after it first sells the securities. The SEC says a Form D does not represent SEC approval and that the SEC does not approve any offering.
- **State law.** The bulletin adds that an offering exempt from SEC registration may still have to comply separately with state securities laws.

The bulletin states that Rule 506 allows an unlimited amount to be raised. Rule 504 allows certain issuers to sell up to $10 million in any 12-month period. Which one fits your round is a question for counsel.

## What the regulator tells investors to ask

The SEC's bulletin is addressed to investors, and it is the nearest thing to a public description of what a careful investor weighs. It says a company in a private placement may be early stage and high risk, that the investor should be able to afford a total loss, that the investment is highly illiquid, and that the company is not required to give the disclosure a registered offering requires. It then lists things for the investor to consider, including:

- whether the company provided financial statements, and whether they are independently audited;
- whether the claims and expectations are reasonable, and how reasonable the reliance is on a particular technology, customer or product claim;
- who the competitors are, and the experience and background of management;
- how long the company has been in business and whether it has made prior offerings;
- how the company plans to use the money raised;
- whether the securities carry transfer restrictions, and whether the investor is comfortable holding them indefinitely.

You can read the list as the questions a careful investor may bring. Having answers ready is the point of the [investor readiness checklist](/fundability/investor-readiness-checklist/) and the [data room guide](/fundability/data-room/). The list is staff advice to investors, which the bulletin says has no legal force or effect, and it is not a description of any particular investor.

## Questions for the investor, and for your own counsel

- Which accredited investor category does this investor, or the vehicle they invest through, rely on, and who checks it?
- Is the offering under Rule 506(b), Rule 506(c) or another exemption, and does that change who I may talk to about it?
- What will the investor want to see before closing, and what will they want afterward?
- If the investor is a fund, what does the fund need to complete an investment, and who inside it decides?
- What filings do I owe, including a Form D and any state filings?
- Which of the questions in [questions to ask your counsel before you sign](/insights/questions-to-ask-your-counsel-before-you-sign/) apply here?
