*This article is general information, not legal or financial advice. It was written on October 10, 2026. It does not say whether you can afford to change your working arrangements or how much money you need. Those are questions for you and for a qualified professional.*

Many companies begin as something done in the evenings, on weekends or between classes. At some point the question arrives: should this become the main thing? For a founder with a job, a degree or both, it is one of the biggest decisions in the early life of the company, and it is often made late at night, on feeling.

This article is about how to make the decision on purpose. It does not tell you when to commit, and no source can. It describes a way to separate the evidence about the work from the facts about your own situation, to write the criteria before you are in the moment, and to take the decision in stages instead of one jump.

## Two decisions, not one

"Should I go full-time?" combines two different questions.

- **Does the work justify more of my time?** This is a question about the problem, the customers and what you have learned. Evidence answers it.
- **Can I take on the cost of giving it more time?** This is a question about your income, obligations, employer, school and risk tolerance. Only you and your professionals can answer it, and this article does not try.

When the two merge, either one can distort the other. A founder who is desperate to leave a job may read thin evidence generously. A founder afraid of leaving may ignore strong evidence. Writing the two questions down separately is a cheap way to see which one is doing the work.

## The evidence question: borrow the idea of a gate

The GOV.UK Service Manual, which describes how UK public services are built, structures work in phases and puts a decision between them. Its guidance on the first phase says that "What you learn during discovery should help you work out whether you want to move forward to the alpha phase," and that "Running an alpha means you've decided that the benefits of looking further into the problem outweigh the cost."

The page sets out when that first phase is done: "Your discovery is finished when you've decided whether or not you want to move on to alpha." It names two factors in the decision. One is whether "there's a viable service you could build that would make it easier for users to do the thing they need to do". The other is whether "it's cost effective to pursue the problem", which it explains as "weighing up how much it'd cost against how much of an improvement you think you could make."

This is a framework from government, and a company is not a government service. But the structure carries over. The decision is about whether to commit more resources to the next phase, and it is made against criteria, not against a mood.

### Write your criteria before you look

Before the next time you are tempted to decide, write down what would count as enough. Make each item something you can check:

- Which customers or potential customers have taken which actions? (A conversation, a shared example, a trial, a commitment to pay.)
- What have you learned about the problem that you did not know at the start?
- What is the smallest version of the product that could test your riskiest assumption? The service manual's assessment guidance says that at an early stage "you only need to prototype enough to test your riskiest assumptions."
- How will you measure whether it is working? The discovery page lists, among the things to have in place before moving on, to "know how you'll measure whether you've been successful."

The [customer discovery interviews](/insights/customer-discovery-interviews-without-false-positives/) article describes how to collect evidence that is hard to misread, and the [validate the problem playbook](/playbooks/validate-the-problem/) puts the steps in order. The [cut line test](/insights/cutting-an-mvp-the-cut-line-test/) and its [worksheet](/library/mvp-cut-line-worksheet/) help you choose the smallest test.

### Set the date as well as the criteria

Choose a decision date and put it in your calendar. Without one, "not yet" is always available, and the part-time arrangement drifts for months with no one having decided anything. With one, you have a deadline for the evidence and a moment to say yes, no or not yet, and why.

### Stopping is an allowed result

The Service Manual says something that founders rarely hear: "It's not a failure to stop at the end of the discovery phase if your research shows that's the best thing to do. In fact, you'll be saving time and money that could be better spent elsewhere." If your criteria are not met, you have learned something, and the decision to keep the project as a side interest, to change direction or to stop is a result and not a defeat.

## The cost question: definitions and questions, not figures

This article does not tell you how much runway you need. It can say what some of the vocabulary means, so that you can ask better questions of a professional.

The U.S. Small Business Administration's page on startup costs says that "It's easy to overlook expenses when you're thinking through a new business idea." It defines the break-even point as "the point at which total cost and total revenue are equal, meaning there is no loss or gain for your small business." And it makes a point that applies to this decision directly: "How you feel is important, but it shouldn't be the base of your business decisions."

For a founder moving from part-time to full-time, the questions to put to an accountant or other qualified professional include:

- What does my household spend each month, and what changes if my income does?
- What would I give up by leaving a job, such as employer benefits, and what would I have to arrange myself?
- What are the company's costs before it has revenue, and who pays them?
- What is the point at which I would stop, and what would I have left?

These are questions, not a checklist of answers. The [metrics calculator](/tools/metrics-calculator/) can help the company's side of the arithmetic once there are customers. It does not do the personal side.

## Take the decision in steps

The choice is not only between everything and nothing. Stages let you commit more as the evidence comes in:

1. **Defined sprint.** Set aside a fixed period of extra time, such as a vacation or a lighter workload, with a stated question to answer by the end.
2. **Reduced commitments.** Ask whether your employer's or school's policies provide for reduced hours or a leave of absence, and what each would mean for you. This is a question for the policy and for a person who can read it.
3. **Full commitment.** This is the step that follows if your written criteria are met and your own side of the question is settled.

If you are employed, remember that your employment agreement may affect what you can build and when. A separate article in this desk covers intellectual property and employment agreements. If you are a student, your school's policies may affect your work, and another article in this desk lists the questions to ask.

## A one-page decision note

Write down, in one page you will date and keep:

- What I believed when I started, and what I believe now.
- What evidence I have, by customer and by action.
- What would change my mind.
- The date of the decision and who I will tell.
- What I will do if the answer is no.

Reread it on the decision date before you decide. It is a record that a late-night mood, in either direction, cannot easily overwrite.

## What this article cannot tell you

This article rests on two pages of UK public-service guidance, one page from a U.S. government small-business site and reasoning. It does not tell you when to go full-time, whether you can afford to, or whether your idea deserves the time. It does not measure how founders who move at different stages fare, and nothing here is evidence that staging the decision improves the outcome. The service manual was written for government teams, so its phases are borrowed as a way of thinking. It does not cover personal finance, taxes, benefits, funding or employment law, which depend on your situation and need qualified advice. Check each source's current text before relying on a quotation.
