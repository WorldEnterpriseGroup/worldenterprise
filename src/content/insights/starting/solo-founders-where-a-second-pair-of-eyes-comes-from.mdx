Founding a company alone has a real advantage: decisions are quick, and nobody has to be persuaded. It has a matching cost. Every decision has exactly one author, and the author is also the only person who would notice that it was wrong. This article is about that gap and about practical ways to close it without taking on a co-founder.

The aim is not company. It is review: someone other than you looking at the work, the plan or the assumption and saying what they see. Review can come from several places, and it can be arranged as a routine instead of left to chance.

## What a team of one is missing

The GOV.UK Service Manual, which sets out how UK public services are built, says that "your delivery team needs to be multidisciplinary and have a range of skills." It also says the team's size and the roles it needs "will change as you build your service." The context is government, and the point carries over. A small company needs people who understand customers, people who can build and people who can decide what comes first, and the mix shifts as the product matures.

A solo founder holds all of those roles at once. That is manageable, but it removes the friction that makes teams good at catching errors. When the builder, the seller and the decision-maker are one person, nobody disagrees with the plan. The risk is not that you lack skill in one area. It is that no one is positioned to tell you which area you are underrating.

## Four sources of a second look

None of these replaces a co-founder. Each covers a different part of the gap, and they cost very different amounts of time and money.

### 1. People who have the problem

Customers, and people who could become customers, are the most important reviewers because they are the only ones who can confirm what the product is for. The service manual puts the rule plainly: "Treat any opinions or suggestions that do not come from users as assumptions that have to be proven by doing research." As a solo founder, your own opinion is the first assumption on that list.

The review you ask of them is narrow. Do not ask whether they like the idea. Ask them to describe how they handle the problem today, show you the workaround, and react to a specific thing you built. The <MaybeLink path="/library/customer-interview-guide/">customer interview guide</MaybeLink> and the article on <MaybeLink path="/insights/customer-discovery-interviews-without-false-positives/">discovery interviews without false positives</MaybeLink> describe how to keep those conversations from steering the answer, and the <MaybeLink path="/playbooks/validate-the-problem/">validate the problem playbook</MaybeLink> puts them in sequence.

### 2. Specialists you hire for one question

Some questions need a professional, and one short engagement is often enough: a lawyer on the founding documents, an accountant on how the business is set up, a security reviewer on the first release. The U.S. Small Business Administration, in its guidance on choosing a business structure, writes: "Consulting with business counselors, attorneys, and accountants can prove helpful." Bring a written question, not a general request. "What are the risks of this arrangement and what would you want in writing?" gives the specialist something to answer.

### 3. Outside reviewers of the work itself

A person who builds the same kind of thing, and who does not depend on your choices, can read your code, your designs or your pricing page and tell you where it confuses them. Hire this by the hour, or ask for a favor that you return. Be specific about what you want: "Read this onboarding flow and tell me where you hesitate" is a better request than "Take a look."

When you are not sure whether to hire a person for a day, a team for a quarter or a freelancer for a task, the <MaybeLink path="/research/dedicated-team-agency-or-freelancers/">research brief on dedicated teams, agencies and freelancers</MaybeLink> sets out the trade-offs, and the <MaybeLink path="/tools/team-composer/">team composer</MaybeLink> helps you list the roles the work needs.

### 4. A standing arrangement

A few founders find a regular exchange with another person who is also building alone: a fixed weekly call, a shared document of decisions, a rule that each of you must challenge one thing. The value comes from the regularity, not the relationship. If it is not on the calendar, it will not happen.

A separate article in this desk covers choosing a co-founder, or deciding not to. Treat that as a decision you make with evidence from the four sources above.

## A review session you can run yourself

The service manual describes how government services are assessed, and the format is worth borrowing because it is designed to produce honest review in a few hours. According to the page, an assessment panel is "normally made up of a lead assessor, a user researcher, a designer, a technical lead", and the page says "the panel is not there to catch you out."

You can build a small version of this for a company of one. Invite two or three people with different strengths, such as someone who knows your customers, someone technical and someone who knows how the business runs. Give them a written agenda and about ninety minutes.

### What to show

The assessment page advises starting with an overview of why you are solving the problem and who the users are, then walking the panel through the user journey. Do the same. Spend the first part saying what the product is for and what you have learned. Spend the second part showing what exists.

The page has two instructions that are useful to a solo founder. The first is "Do not just focus on the happy path." Show what happens when a customer cannot log in, cannot pay, or does not fit the case you designed for. The second is that at an early stage, "you only need to prototype enough to test your riskiest assumptions." Name your riskiest assumption before the session, and ask the reviewers to attack it.

### What to ask

Give the reviewers three standing questions:

1. What did you hear that I am treating as settled when it is only an opinion?
2. What would you want to see before you believed this works?
3. What am I not asking about?

Then stop talking. The page also warns against over-preparing: "Do just enough to make sure you can give an open and honest account." A polished presentation invites applause, and applause does not help you.

### What to do with the result

The assessment page grades each service as green, amber or red, and says: "Not meeting the Service Standard does not mean that you failed." You do not need a formal grade, but a simple version helps. After the session, write down each concern and mark it as one of three things: fix before the next release, fix by a date you choose, or consciously accepted. Keep the list. At the next session, start with it.

## A rhythm that survives a busy month

Review that depends on motivation stops when the work gets busy. Some ways to keep it going:

- Put a recurring session on the calendar, with the reviewers' names next to each date.
- Keep a single running document of decisions, each with the reason and the date, so a reviewer can read it in ten minutes.
- When you change direction, write down what you believed before and what changed your mind.
- Rotate who you ask. A reviewer who has seen the product for six months has stopped seeing it.

None of this is expensive. It is a habit that substitutes scheduled disagreement for the disagreement a co-founder would provide.

## What this article cannot tell you

This article rests on three pages of UK public-service guidance, one passage from a U.S. government small-business guide, and reasoning. It does not measure whether solo founders who use outside review do better than those who do not, and nothing here is evidence that they do. The assessment format was written for government services, so borrowing it is an adaptation, not a validated method for startups. The article does not tell you how many reviewers to use, whom to trust, what to pay a specialist or whether to stay solo. Those depend on your product, your market and your finances. It does not cover fundraising, equity or employment questions, which need counsel and are covered elsewhere. Check each source's current text before relying on a quotation.
