This playbook does not give legal, tax or investment advice, and it does not tell you how to raise money. It helps you prepare your evidence and your questions, so your own counsel can advise you before you speak with an investor. WE makes no promise of funding.

## What good looks like

Good preparation means an investor can check what you say. Each claim in your pitch points to a record, and your counsel has told you what you can say and to whom.

- Each claim about customers or growth has a source record that you can produce.
- Your metrics use the written definitions from your own sheet, and they are the same in every document.
- Your cap table matches your counsel's records, and you can explain every line.
- You can explain your runway and burn, and the assumptions behind them, without a spreadsheet in hand.
- Your list of investors has a reason for each name, and your update record shows regular progress.

<Callout title="Illustrative example, not a real company">

A founder of a tool that schedules maintenance for small fleets of delivery vans has a few paying customers and a monthly review. Before she contacts anyone, she compares customer revenue with outside investment and decides what each would ask of her. She orders her records in a folder with an index. She writes her runway calculation and its assumptions on one page, and she lists her questions for her lawyer about instruments and about what she may say to whom. Her short list names five investors who work with early software companies, with a reason beside each. Her lawyer answers the questions before she sends the first message.

</Callout>

## Questions to ask your counsel

These are questions to bring, not answers. The answers depend on your company, your investors and where each of you is based.

- Which instruments are open to a company like ours, and what does each one change for the founders?
- Which rules apply when we look for investment, and what must we do before we speak with an investor?
- What may we say about our company and our plans, and to whom?
- Does each investor we want to approach need to meet any condition before we can talk with them?
- What should we put in writing, and what should we hold back until an agreement is signed?
- What will an investor ask to see about our contracts, our code and our agreements, and are our records ready?

## What a public regulator says

The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) says that every offer and sale of securities must either be registered under the Securities Act of 1933 or rely on an available exemption from registration. Whether and how that applies to your company is a question for your counsel. Ask it before you speak with an investor, not after.

## Evidence to check before you move on

This is the last playbook in the chain. You are ready to begin conversations when you can show each of these. The <MaybeLink path="/fundability/">investor readiness section</MaybeLink> holds the guides on data rooms and updates.

- The route comparison is written and shows why outside investment fits.
- The evidence pack is ordered against an index, and each folder is complete or marked not applicable.
- Metrics, runway and burn have written definitions and assumptions.
- Your counsel has answered your questions in writing.
- The investor list has a reason for each name, and the time budget is written.
