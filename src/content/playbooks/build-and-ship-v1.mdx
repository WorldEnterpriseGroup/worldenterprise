## What good looks like

A good first release is small, working and watched. The team can say what shipped this week, what moved below the cut line and why, and the people using it are people you can call.

- Each week ends with a written review that compares what was built with the cut line.
- A thin slice of the riskiest path worked early, so the hardest problem was found while it was still cheap.
- The release checklist exists, carries dates and has been followed at least once, including the security and accessibility items.
- A person outside your team reached the first result without you doing it for them.
- You can undo a release. The way back to the last good version was decided before the release, not during it.

<Callout title="Illustrative example, not a real company">

A founder building a shift-swap tool for restaurants works with a small remote team across time zones. Each Monday the team writes one goal for the week in a shared document, and each Friday the founder compares what was built with the cut line. In the second week the review shows that a calendar export crept in. The founder moves it below the line and writes why. The first release goes to a few restaurant managers she has spoken with by name. One of them cannot find the swap button, and the note from that session becomes the top backlog item.

</Callout>

## Standards to build against

A checklist is stronger when each line points to a public standard instead of a habit. Two are worth putting on the first list.

The W3C's Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2 cover a wide range of recommendations for making web content more accessible, and the document is a W3C Recommendation dated 12 December 2024. Put its success criteria on the release checklist in the first week, because fixing accessibility after launch touches everything that is already built.

NIST's Secure Software Development Framework, published as SP 800-218, describes a core set of high-level secure software development practices that can be integrated into each SDLC implementation. Use it as a vocabulary for the security items on your checklist, and write down which practices you have adopted and which you have deferred.

Neither document tells you that your product is accessible or secure. They give you a shared list to check against and to show to a reviewer.

## Evidence to check before you move on

Move to <MaybeLink path="/playbooks/hire-your-first-team-member/">Hire your first team member</MaybeLink> when you can show each of these.

- A person outside your team reached the first result without you doing it for them.
- The release checklist exists, has dates on it and has been followed at least once.
- Weekly review notes show what changed in scope and why.
- The backlog is ordered, and each of the top items points to something you observed.

Version one will be incomplete. That is expected. What matters is that you can say exactly what is missing and what you saw that told you so.
