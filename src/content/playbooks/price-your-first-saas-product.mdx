description: Tie price to the value a customer gets, choose a simple pricing model, test it in real conversations and record every reaction before you publish a page.
steps:
  - title: Write down the value in the customer's terms
    body: State what the customer gains when the product works, in their words and units, such as hours saved, errors avoided or revenue protected. Price follows value. It does not follow your costs.
  - title: Find out what the customer does today and what it costs them
    body: List the current alternatives, including spreadsheets, staff time and other tools, and what each costs the customer in money and effort. That range sets the frame for your price.
  - title: Choose a pricing model you can explain in one sentence
    body: Pick what you charge for, such as seats, usage or a flat package, based on how the customer gets value. Write the sentence you would say to a buyer.
  - title: Test the price in conversations before you publish it
    body: Raise price with each prospect early and write down what they said. A reaction is not a no. Ask what they would compare it with and what would make it easy to say yes.
  - title: Design a small set of packages
    body: Offer a few packages that differ in the value they give, with names a buyer can tell apart. Remove any option that nobody can explain a reason to choose.
  - title: Publish the page and review it on a schedule
    body: Put the price on a page that states what is included and what is not. Set a regular date to review the log of reactions and to change the price or the packages when the evidence calls for it.

This playbook does not recommend a price or a range, because the right price depends on your customers and your evidence. It shows how to find your own.

## What good looks like

A good first price rests on conversations, not on a competitor's page. You can say what the customer gains, what they pay today to get it done, and why the price you chose fits between the two.

- The value statement uses the customer's own words and units, taken from your interviews and pilots.
- You know what customers do today and what it costs them, in money, time and effort.
- The pricing model fits in one sentence, and a buyer can repeat it back.
- Your log holds the price reactions of real prospects, including the nos and the silences.
- The pricing page says what is included and what is not, and each package has a reason to exist.

<Callout title="Illustrative example, not a real company">

A founder builds a tool that helps small property managers collect maintenance requests. In her interviews, managers described spending hours each week chasing tenants by phone and text. She writes the value as time spent chasing requests, not as features. She tells each prospect a price early and notes the reaction. Two managers say the price compares well with a part-time assistant, and one says the unit of charge confuses him. She changes what she charges for from a count of users to a count of properties, because that is how her buyers think of their work. She writes the change and the reason in the log.

</Callout>
