stage: build

title: Design the product
description: "Turn a validated problem into a version-one design: the shortest path to value, a written cut line and the decisions you will defer."
steps:
  - title: List the jobs the customer is hiring you to do
    body: Write each job as a sentence from the customer's side, using the words they used in your interviews. These sentences, not a feature wish list, are the brief for the design.
  - title: Map the shortest path to value
    body: Draw the single route from first visit to the moment the customer gets what they came for. Remove every step that is not on that route.
  - title: Sketch the key screens and test them on paper
    body: Show low-detail sketches to people from your interviews and ask what they expect to happen next. Fix the confusion now, while a fix costs a pencil line.
  - title: Draw the cut line
    body: Sort every idea into three groups, which are must exist for the first customer to get value, can wait, and will not build. Version one is everything above the line and nothing below it.
  - title: Choose well-understood components
    body: Use proven parts for sign-in, billing and email unless one of them is your product. Keep a short log of the choices you are deliberately deferring and the trigger that would reopen each.
  - title: Write acceptance criteria for what is above the line
    body: For each item, write the observable result that means it is done. A person who did not design it should be able to check it.
artifacts:
  - A list of customer jobs in the customer's own words
  - A path to first value, drawn from first visit to first result
  - Annotated screen sketches
  - A written cut line for version one
  - A log of deferred decisions
  - Acceptance criteria for every item above the line

## What good looks like

A good design is specific enough that a team can build from it without guessing, and small enough that the first customer can reach the result in one sitting. It does not have to be finished or beautiful.

- Every item above the cut line traces back to a job a customer described in their own words.
- The path to first value fits on one page, and there is no step on it that a customer would call optional.
- The cut line is written down, and the items below it have a stated reason for waiting.
- Each item above the line has an acceptance criterion that someone who did not design it could check.
- Choices you are deferring sit in a log with the trigger that would reopen each one.

<Callout title="Illustrative example, not a real company">

A founder building invoice reminders for small design studios has a long list of ideas: recurring invoices, a client portal, tax reports, a mobile app and reminder emails. The job she heard in interviews was getting paid without an awkward conversation. Her cut line keeps three items above it: create an invoice, send a reminder on a schedule, and see which invoices are overdue. Everything else goes below the line with a note. The acceptance criterion for reminders reads: a studio owner sets a schedule once and a reminder arrives on that date without further action. A friend who has never seen the sketches can check it.

</Callout>

## Decision aid: above the line or below it

Run each candidate feature through these questions in order. The first question you answer no to decides where the feature goes.

  caption="Questions that place a feature above or below the cut line"
  columns={["Question", "If the answer is yes", "If the answer is no"]}
  rows={[
    ["Did a customer describe the job this feature does, in their own words?", "Keep it on the list and ask the next question.", "Below the line. Write down which interview would change your mind."],
    ["Is the path to first value broken without it?", "Above the line.", "Ask the next question."],
    ["Can the job be done by hand for the first customers?", "Below the line. Do it by hand and watch what happens.", "Ask the next question."],
    ["Can a proven component do the job, such as sign-in, billing or email?", "Buy or adopt it, and place the choice in the deferred-decisions log.", "Above the line, with an acceptance criterion."],
    ["Would you be embarrassed to release without it?", "Check whether the embarrassment is about the customer or about you. Only the first belongs above the line.", "Below the line."]

## Evidence to check before you move on

Move to <MaybeLink path="/playbooks/set-up-the-company/">Set up the company</MaybeLink> when you can show each of these. The company setup comes before you build because the people who write code for you will be asked who owns it.

- Every item above the cut line traces back to a customer job.
- The path to first value fits on one page and has no step that a customer would call optional.
- Someone outside your team has read the acceptance criteria and could tell whether a feature passes.
- Deferred decisions are listed with the trigger that would bring each one back.

The design does not have to be finished or beautiful. It has to be specific enough that a team can build from it without guessing.
