steps:
  - title: Write the problem brief
    body: State who has the problem, what they do about it today and why that is not good enough, on one page. If you cannot write it plainly, you are not ready to interview anyone.
  - title: List the assumptions that would sink the idea
    body: Separate what you know from what you hope. Rank each assumption by how badly the idea fails if it is wrong, then test the top ones first.
  - title: Find people who have the problem
    body: Recruit from the group your brief describes, not from friends. Prefer people who dealt with the problem recently, because recent behavior is better evidence than opinion.
  - title: Interview about the past, not the future
    body: Ask what happened the last time, what they tried and what it cost them. Keep your solution out of the conversation. Write notes the same day, in the same format every time.
  - title: Look for commitment, not compliments
    body: Praise costs nothing. Evidence is time, access, money or a written pilot commitment. Record each person's response honestly, including a plain no.
  - title: Decide, and write the decision down
    body: Set what you learned against your assumptions. Choose to continue, change the problem or stop, and write the evidence behind the choice so someone else can read it.

## What good looks like

Validation is finished when a stranger could read your notes and reach the same conclusion you did. That is a higher bar than "people liked the idea", and it is the bar that protects your time.

- The problem brief names one kind of person, one situation and one thing they do about it today. It does not name a market.
- You have spoken with people who match the brief and do not know you, and every conversation is written down in the same format on the same day.
- Some of those people have given you something that costs them, such as an introduction, access to their data, a second conversation or a written commitment to try a pilot.
- You wrote down what would make you stop before the first interview, and you can say whether that line was crossed.
- The decision to continue, change the problem or stop is in writing, with the evidence beside it.

<Callout title="Illustrative example, not a real company">

A founder with years of experience in dental-practice administration wants to build scheduling software. The first brief says that dental practices want better scheduling. After a round of interviews with office managers, the notes say something narrower. The pain is a canceled appointment that nobody fills in time, and two people described a spreadsheet they rebuild every Monday to track it. The founder rewrites the brief around late-filled cancellations, drops the idea of a full scheduling system and asks both spreadsheet users for a second conversation. Both agree. The written decision reads: change the problem, then continue with the narrower one.

</Callout>

## Evidence to check before you move on

Move to <MaybeLink path="/playbooks/design-the-product/">Design the product</MaybeLink> when each of these is true and you could show it to a stranger.

- The problem brief reads clearly to someone outside your field in about two minutes.
- Every assumption has a status: supported, contradicted or still untested.
- Interview notes exist for every conversation, in one format, with the date.
- The decision to continue, change or stop is written down with the evidence beside it.

A "stop" is a useful result. It costs far less than building the wrong product, and your notes still show how you think.
