An investor update is a short written report on how the company is doing, sent on a regular rhythm to people who follow it. Its first job is to keep people informed. Its second job, for the founder, is to create a dated record of progress that is hard to fake and easy to share later.

You do not need investors to start. Send updates to advisers, design partners and anyone who has given you time or advice. A run of dated updates shows what you did, in what order and what you learned from it.

## Pick a rhythm and keep it

Weekly and monthly both work. What matters is that you choose a rhythm you can keep, send on the same day, and use the same structure every time. A short update sent on time is worth more than a long one sent when you remember.

## Habits that make updates worth reading

- **Tell bad news early.** A problem reported while it is small is a sign of control. The same problem found later looks like concealment.
- **Define your numbers once.** Write down what revenue, churn and activation mean for your company, link to the definitions, and never change them silently. If you must change one, say so and show both figures for the period. The [early-stage SaaS metrics brief](/research/early-stage-saas-metrics-definitions/) sets out common definitions you can adapt.
- **Keep it short.** If it takes longer than a few minutes to read, shorten it. Put detail in an attachment.
- **Label estimates.** Say which numbers are measured, which are estimated and which are plans.
- **Make one clear ask.** Many readers want to help and do not know how.

## Care with statements about the future

An update that talks about future results can be read as a statement to people who may put money into your company. Describe plans as plans, give the assumptions behind them and avoid anything that sounds like a promise. Ask your own counsel how to word forward-looking statements, and what you should and should not say to people who are not yet investors.

## Where the update lives
