Sooner or later a customer, or an investor doing diligence, sends a spreadsheet of questions about how your company protects data. Most are yes or no questions with a box for comments. A small company can answer them well, and the way to do it is not to sound large. A good answer is true, specific and provable, and you can write one for every common question before anyone asks.

This guide is about the answering, not the security program itself. It assumes you have started on the basics and want to describe them accurately. It makes no claim about the security of any product of World Enterprise, and it is not legal advice.

## Why the questions arrive

A customer who hands part of its work to a supplier takes on a risk it cannot see from outside, so it asks. The American Institute of Certified Public Accountants describes its System and Organization Controls reports as ones that [provide users with valuable information that is needed to assess and address the risks associated with outsourcing services](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/resources/landing/system-and-organization-controls-soc-suite-of-services). A questionnaire is the lighter version of the same need. It lets a buyer compare suppliers without an audit.

Knowing that helps you answer. The reader is not trying to catch you out. The reader is deciding how much trust to place in a supplier it cannot inspect, and a straight answer, even a weak one, gives them something to work with.

## Formats you may meet

Questionnaires vary, but a few formats come up often.

- **A customer's own spreadsheet.** The most common form. The questions are the customer's, and so is the order.
- **A standard question set.** The Cloud Security Alliance publishes a control framework for cloud computing, the Cloud Controls Matrix, and says the questionnaire built into it [offers a simple set of "yes/no" questions to assess cloud providers](https://cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/cloud-controls-matrix). Some buyers ask suppliers to fill in that set instead of writing their own.
- **A request for an independent report.** The AICPA describes its System and Organization Controls as [a suite of service offerings CPAs may provide](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/topic/audit-assurance/audit-and-assurance-greater-than-soc-2). A buyer may ask whether you have one. If you do not, say so plainly and answer the questionnaire instead. Never imply you hold a report you do not.
- **A framework mapping.** The National Institute of Standards and Technology describes its Cybersecurity Framework as [helping organizations to better understand and improve their management of cybersecurity risk](https://www.nist.gov/cyberframework). A buyer may ask how your practices line up with it. A small company can answer by describing what it does in its own words.

## Build an answer library

The work of answering is mostly the same work each time, so do it once and keep it.

1. **Start from your data inventory.** Write down every system that holds customer data and who can reach it. The [security baseline checklist](/library/security-baseline-checklist/) and the [research brief on a SaaS security baseline](/research/saas-security-baseline-year-one/) cover what to put in place and record.
2. **Group the questions by topic.** Access, data, vendors, incidents, people and development cover most questionnaires. One answer per topic, reused, keeps your replies consistent.
3. **Attach evidence to each answer.** A policy, a screenshot, a setting or a log that a reviewer could open. If an answer has no evidence, mark it.
4. **Give every answer an owner and a date.** Someone is responsible for keeping each one true, and the date shows when it was last checked.
5. **Review after every change.** A new vendor, a new employee with access or a new product feature can make an old answer wrong.

## Use a small set of honest statuses

Yes and no are not the only honest answers. A set of statuses keeps you from rounding up.

Notice that the weakest answers are also the most specific. A reader can act on "not yet required for the support inbox", and cannot act on a bare yes.

## Rules that keep answers honest

- **Answer the question asked.** If the question is about encryption of stored data, do not answer with a paragraph about your office.
- **Never round up.** A plan is a plan. A pilot is a pilot. A policy nobody follows is not a control.
- **Keep the answers the same across customers.** A reader who sees the same question answered two ways will trust neither.
- **Do not claim a certification or report you do not hold.** State what you have, in the words the issuer uses.
- **Say who is accountable.** An answer with a named owner is easier to rely on than an anonymous one.
- **Ask when you are unsure.** If a question uses a term you do not know, ask the sender what it means before you reply. Your counsel can help with any question about legal duties.
