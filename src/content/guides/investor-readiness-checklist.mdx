Investors and their advisers check claims. They ask whether the problem is real, whether people use the product, whether the numbers mean what they say and whether the company owns what it sells. A company that can answer with documents, rather than assurances, makes that check quick.

This checklist turns those questions into twelve statements. Each one is something you can answer yes or no, and each one has an artifact: a document, a log or a record that another person could open. If you cannot produce the artifact, treat the statement as not yet true.

## How to use the checklist

1. Read each statement and answer it honestly. "Mostly" is a no.
2. For every yes, find the artifact and put it somewhere you can share it. If you cannot find it, change the answer.
3. For every no, follow the link in the last column. Each one points to a playbook, a template or a guide that helps you build the missing piece.
4. Return in a month. The list is meant to shrink.

The [self-check on the investor readiness page](/fundability/#self-check) runs the same twelve statements in your browser. It stores nothing and never produces a score.

## What counts as an artifact

An artifact is a record that stands on its own. Four tests help you decide whether yours does:

- **A stranger can open it.** It does not depend on you being in the room to explain it.
- **It is dated.** The date shows whether it describes the company today or a year ago.
- **It says what it is.** The file name and the first line state its purpose.
- **It lives in one place.** If the answer is "somewhere in my email", the statement is not yet true.

A document that fails one of these is not worthless. It is a draft of an artifact, and the fix is usually an hour of tidying rather than new work.

## Where a yes turns into a no

Most founders who work through the list find the same few statements slipping. These are the patterns to look for.

- **Interviews without a summary.** Notes exist, but each conversation is written down differently and nothing says what the pile shows. The guide on [customer interview synthesis](/fundability/customer-interview-synthesis/) turns a set of notes into one page of findings.
- **Interest counted as commitment.** The customer log records who said they liked the product, not what each person agreed to do or pay. Record the commitment, or say that there was none.
- **Metrics that move.** A measure was calculated one way in March and another way in June. Write the definition down once and keep it. The [metrics calculator](/tools/metrics-calculator/) shows a consistent way to compute the common ones.
- **Ownership by memory.** Someone wrote code for the company and everyone agrees the company owns it, but nothing was signed. The U.S. Copyright Act says a transfer of copyright ownership [is not valid unless an instrument of conveyance](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/204), or a note or memorandum of the transfer, is in writing and signed by the owner or the owner's authorized agent. Ask your counsel how to close the gap, and keep the result in the [seed data room](/fundability/data-room/).
- **A data list that is really a feeling.** "We keep it in a few places" is not an inventory. Write down each system that holds customer data and who can reach it. NIST describes version 2.0 of its Cybersecurity Framework as one that [is designed for all audiences, industry sectors and organization types](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2024/02/nist-releases-version-20-landmark-cybersecurity-framework), so a small company can borrow its vocabulary without adopting the whole framework. The guide on [security questionnaire answers](/fundability/security-questionnaire-answers/) shows how to turn the inventory into answers.
- **One update, once.** A single investor update shows a habit that has not started. Two or more, in the same format, show one that has. The guide on [investor updates](/fundability/investor-updates/) gives the structure.

## About the evidence

Two outside sources back the statements in this guide, both read on the dates listed under Sources: the text of 17 U.S.C. section 204 on how a copyright transfer is made, and NIST's description of its Cybersecurity Framework 2.0. The rest is working practice. The twelve statements are a preparation aid built from common diligence questions, and no public standard defines them. Treat them as a way to find gaps, not as a test you can pass.

## What the checklist is not

It is not a rating, and finishing it does not make a raise likely. Whether anyone invests depends on the market, the founders, the timing and the investor, and none of those are on this list. The checklist only helps you make your evidence easy to check.

It is also not legal advice. Several items, including ownership records, assignments of work and anything you sign, are matters for your own counsel. The guide on [the seed data room](/fundability/data-room/) shows where those records belong, and the guide on [SAFEs in plain language](/fundability/safe-explained/) lists questions to bring to your lawyer.
