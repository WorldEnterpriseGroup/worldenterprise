### early-stage-diligence: body sentences

1. This brief is for an investor who is deciding how much to look at before engaging with a young software company, and how much weight each thing deserves.
2. It lists the records such a company can show, says what a public source reports about the rule behind each record, and then states what the record cannot establish.
3. It is general information, current as of October 11, 2026.
4. It is not legal, tax or investment advice, and every rule it describes should be checked against the current text with a qualified professional before a decision rests on it.
5. A company at the earliest stage has little history to examine.
6. It may have no audited accounts, a handful of customers and a product that changed twice in the last quarter.
7. An investor who asks for "the diligence materials" will receive a folder, and the folder will be a mixture of documents with very different standing: a register that the law requires the company to keep, a contract that a court would read, a slide that the founder wrote last week.
8. The useful question is not how large the folder is.
9. It is what each item in it can prove, who produced it, and what would have to be true for it to be wrong.
10. A document an investor can read tells them what the company has recorded, and only a separate check tells them whether the record is true.
11. This brief sorts the evidence by the kind of claim it supports.
12. It covers six records: who owns the shares, who owns the work, how the shares were sold, what tax elections the founders made, how the product was built and secured, and what open source code sits inside it.
13. It then turns to the numbers a company reports about itself, where public sources are thinnest.
14. It ends with questions to put to the company and a plain statement of what the exercise cannot show.
15. This brief draws on ten public documents, each retrieved and read on the day it was written, October 11, 2026.
16. They fall into four groups.
17. The first group is statute and regulation, read as published by the Legal Information Institute at Cornell Law School and by the State of Delaware: the definition of an accredited investor in the Securities and Exchange Commission's Regulation D, the transfer rules for copyright and patents in sections 204 of title 17 and 261 of title 35, the Treasury regulation on the election under section 83 of the Internal Revenue Code, section 1202 of the Internal Revenue Code and section 219 of the Delaware General Corporation Law.
18. They state the rule as the text states it.
19. They do not say how a rule applies to any company.
20. The second group is agency material for a general audience: the Securities and Exchange Commission's page on private placements under Rule 506(b) and the Federal Trade Commission's security guide for business.
21. A summary page leaves out exceptions that the underlying rule contains.
22. The third group is two documents from bodies that publish a method or a definition: the National Institute of Standards and Technology's Secure Software Development Framework and the Open Source Initiative's Open Source Definition.
23. They describe a practice or a term.
24. Neither one certifies anything about a company.
25. The fourth group is the analysis in this brief itself.
26. Where a sentence is a judgment and not a report of a source, it is marked as analysis or phrased as a consideration.
27. The analysis is not drawn from a study of outcomes, and no statistic about how often any company succeeds appears here, because none of the ten sources supplies one.
28. Three limits on the sources belong at the start.
29. They describe United States federal rules and one state's corporate statute, so every other jurisdiction is outside them.
30. They were read on a single day, so a changed rule would not appear.
31. And none of them speaks to the numbers a company reports about its customers and revenue, which is why that section below is analysis and cites no source.
32. Treat each item as one of three kinds.
33. The first kind is a record the law or a contract makes the company keep, such as the register of stockholders or a signed assignment.
34. Its standing is high, because a wrong entry has legal consequences.
35. The second kind is a filing or election the company made with a government office.
36. It shows what the company chose to say to that office, on a date, and no more.
37. The third kind is a statement the company makes about itself: a deck, a spreadsheet, a dashboard.
38. Its standing is the standing of whoever wrote it.
39. Analysis: the three kinds fail in different ways.
40. A record can be incomplete, because a transfer was never entered.
41. A filing can be accurate and still beside the point, because it was made for a different purpose than the one the investor has in mind.
42. A statement can be sincere and wrong.
43. A reader who names which kind of failure each item is exposed to will ask better follow-up questions than one who asks whether it is "clean."
44. The first thing to establish is who holds the shares.
45. In the Delaware statute, the stock ledger is defined as records administered by or on behalf of the corporation, and the statute says the names of the stockholders of record, their addresses, the number of shares registered in each name and all issuances and transfers of stock are recorded in it.
46. The same section says that the stock ledger is the only evidence as to who the stockholders entitled to examine the list or to vote are.
47. Two consequences follow for a reader.
48. The first is that a spreadsheet of holdings, often called a cap table, is not the statutory record.
49. It is a working document that may or may not agree with the ledger.
50. The second is that the ledger is evidence of who holds shares of record, which is a narrower thing than who is entitled to what under every side agreement.
51. An option, a promise of shares for future work or a convertible instrument can change the real ownership without changing a line of the ledger.
52. Analysis: the useful check is to reconcile the three documents the company can produce, the ledger, the cap table and the signed instruments, and to ask the company to explain each difference.
53. Early-stage companies often keep these three loosely, and a difference does not by itself suggest anything improper.
54. A difference that nobody can explain is a finding about the company's record keeping, and it can be put to counsel as one.
55. This section reports Delaware law and no other.
56. A company formed elsewhere is governed by its own state's statute, which this brief does not read.
57. For a software company the most valuable asset is usually code, and the records of who owns it are the contracts that moved it.
58. Two statutes show the shape of the rule.
59. For copyright, the law says a transfer of copyright ownership, other than by operation of law, is not valid unless an instrument of conveyance, or a note or memorandum of the transfer, is in writing and signed by the owner of the rights conveyed or the owner's duly authorized agent.
60. For patents, the law says applications for patent, patents, or any interest in them, are assignable in law by an instrument in writing.
61. The patent statute adds a rule about recording: an assignment is void as against a subsequent purchaser unless it is recorded in the Patent and Trademark Office no later than three months from its date, with a further condition about the date of the later purchase that a reader should take from the statute itself.
62. Two further sentences are analysis.
63. First, the writing requirement means that a founder who wrote the first version before the company existed may still hold the rights in it, unless a signed document moved them.
64. A statement that "the company owns its code" is therefore a claim about paperwork.
65. Second, the contributors who matter are not only the founders.
66. Contractors, early collaborators and anyone who wrote code before an employment agreement was signed each need a document, and the signed copy is the evidence.
67. A reader can ask for the agreement each contributor signed, the date it was signed relative to the first contribution, and the list of contributors drawn from the source repository history.
68. The comparison of the two lists is the check.
69. A name in the history with no matching agreement is a gap, and the cure for a gap is a signed document, which counsel can describe.
70. What the record cannot show is whether the code does what the company says it does, or whether someone else holds a patent that the product infringes.
71. Ownership of a thing and freedom to use it are separate questions.
72. A company that sold shares to investors had to fit each sale within a registration or an exemption from it.
73. The Securities and Exchange Commission's page on Rule 506(b) says that a company relying on it can sell securities to an unlimited number of accredited investors and that securities may not be sold to more than 35 non-accredited investors in any 90-calendar day period.
74. It says the company must give any non-accredited investors the financial statement information specified in Rule 506, that there may be no general solicitation or advertising to market the securities, and that the company must file a notice on Form D no later than 15 days after the first sale of securities in the offering.
75. The regulation defines who an accredited investor is.
76. Its lead-in says that an accredited investor is any person who comes within any of the listed categories, or whom the issuer reasonably believes comes within them, at the time of the sale of the securities to that person.
77. Several of the categories for natural persons turn on income or net worth, and the regulation states the thresholds in its text, which a reader should consult directly and not rely on a summary for.
78. What does this let a reader check?
79. Three things.
80. Whether a Form D was filed, and when, relative to the first sale.
81. Whether the company can say which exemption it relied on for each round.
82. And whether the company kept the basis for its belief that each purchaser was accredited.
83. The phrase "reasonably believes" in the regulation makes that last item a matter of what the company recorded at the time.
84. What the record cannot show is that the exemption was in fact available.
85. A filed notice is a statement to the Commission, not a finding by it.
86. Analysis: a missing notice is not proof that an offering was improper, and a filed one is not proof that it was proper.
87. Both are inputs to a conversation with counsel about whether the company's past sales leave any exposure.
88. State securities requirements also apply to many offerings and are outside this brief.
89. When a founder receives shares that are subject to vesting, the tax rules treat the receipt in a particular way unless the founder makes an election.
90. The Treasury regulation on the election under section 83 says the election must be filed no later than 30 days after the date the property was transferred, and may be filed before the date of transfer.
91. It says the election is made by filing one copy of a written statement with the internal revenue office where the person who performed the services files tax documents.
92. The regulation names a deadline and a form of filing.
93. It does not say what the election was worth to any individual, and this brief does not either.
94. A reader can ask whether each founder made the election and when, and can ask to see the filing and the proof that it was sent.
95. Analysis: because the deadline is short, whether it was met is a matter of fact that is better known early than late.
96. The consequences for a given person belong with that person's tax adviser.
97. A second provision that investors often hear about is section 1202.
98. Its text provides for the exclusion of 50 percent of any gain from the sale or exchange of qualified small business stock acquired on or before the applicable date and held for more than five years, and it defines a qualified small business as a domestic corporation which is a C corporation, among other conditions.
99. The statute has further conditions beyond those quoted, and the page read for this brief shows the text of the section and not a determination about any company.
100. A reader should treat any statement that a company's shares qualify as a claim for counsel to confirm and not as a fact.
101. A company's security practice is something an investor can ask about, and there are public frameworks that give the questions a vocabulary.
102. The National Institute of Standards and Technology publication on the Secure Software Development Framework describes itself as recommending a core set of high-level secure software development practices that can be integrated into each software development life cycle implementation.
103. It says that few software development life cycle models explicitly address software security in detail, and it gives purchasers and suppliers a shared vocabulary for talking about the practices.
104. The Federal Trade Commission's guide for business names ten lessons.
105. They include controlling access to data sensibly, requiring secure passwords and authentication, applying sound security practices when developing new products, and making sure service providers implement reasonable security measures.
106. The guide opens with the statement that when managing a network, developing an app, or even organizing paper files, sound security is no accident.
107. Neither document is a standard that a company passes.
108. Neither one certifies the company, and a company that says it follows either of them is making a claim that the reader can test by asking for the practice.
109. Analysis: useful questions are concrete ones.
110. Who can reach production data and how is that access reviewed?
111. How does a code change reach production, and who approves it?
112. How would the company learn that a dependency it uses had a newly published vulnerability?
113. A founder who can answer those in specific terms has told the investor more than one who produces a policy document.
114. The evidence cannot show that the product is secure.
115. It shows what practices exist on the day of the question.
116. A product that was reviewed last quarter has since had new code added to it, and the reader should ask how the practice keeps pace.
117. Most software products include code written by others and licensed for reuse, and the terms of those licenses differ.
118. The Open Source Initiative's Open Source Definition begins with the statement that open source does not just mean access to the source code, and it lists criteria that a license must meet to be called open source, among them redistribution terms, the availability of source code and the permission of derived works.
119. Analysis: a license that meets the definition can still carry obligations, and the definition does not list them.
120. A reader can ask for a list of the open source components the product includes, the license of each and how the company tracks them.
121. Analysis: the question that matters most for a software company is whether any component carries an obligation that would reach the company's own code if the product were distributed in a particular way, and the answer depends on the specific license and on how the component is used.
122. That is a matter for the company's counsel, and an investor can fairly ask whether anyone has done the review.
123. The definition is a description of a term.
124. It says nothing about any component, and a statement that a product "uses only permissive licenses" is, again, a claim the company must support with its list.
125. This section cites no source, and it is analysis from start to finish.
126. The sources read for this brief do not address revenue, customer counts or retention, and a reader should not take any figure in a company's deck as having the standing of a record.

128. A company that reports monthly recurring revenue has chosen what counts as recurring, what counts as a month and how to treat a discount or a pause.
129. Two companies reporting the same label may mean different things, and the same company may change its meaning between two reports.

136. A short history may reflect a launch effect, a founder's personal network or a single contract, and a reader should be slow to read a trend into a few data points.
